The Geology of Ethiopia and the Horn

🔍 2.1 Introduction to Geology

Geology is the scientific study of the Earth, focusing on its origin, materials composition, evolution, and the internal/external processes acting upon it.

  • It is concerned with events that took place in the remote past when no human witness existed and with features far beneath the Earth's surface where no one can see directly.
  • A great deal of geological understanding is obtained by inference from what can be observed and measured.
📌 Methods of Geological Study:
  • Direct observation: Studying rocks and landforms at the Earth's surface.
  • Indirect methods:
    • Geophysics: Studying earthquake waves that penetrate deep beneath the surface.
    • Geochemistry: Detailed analysis of chemical composition to determine rock origins.
    • Geochronology: Determining the numerical ages of rocks using radioactive elements.

🌏 Continental Drift Theory

The Earth's landmasses were once assembled together in a single huge supercontinent called Pangaea. German Climatologist Alfred Wegener proposed the Continental Drift Theory.

  • Pangaea first split into two supercontinents:
    • Gondwanaland: The southern landmass (which included Africa).
    • Laurasia: The northern landmass.
  • Later, these split into smaller fragments over millions of years and drifted to form the present arrangement of continents.
💡 Wegener’s Principal Observations (Evidence):
  1. Fit of the Continents: Opposing coastlines fit together like puzzle pieces (best observed along continental shelf margins).
  2. Match of Mountain Belts & Rock Types: Reassembled mountain ranges in West Africa align with North America, Greenland, and Western Europe.
  3. Distribution of Fossils: Identical plant and animal fossil patterns link separated continents (e.g., Mesosaurus in Brazil and South Africa).
  4. Paleoclimates: 200-million-year-old glacial deposits match across India, Australia, South America, and Southern Africa.

⚡ 2.2 Geologic Processes: Endogenic and Exogenic Forces

Geology studies how Earth's materials, structures, processes, and organisms change over time. These processes are categorized into two major forces:

🌋 1. Internal (Endogenic) Processes

  • Driven by internal energy inside the Earth.
  • Includes volcanic activity and tectonic processes:
    • Folding & Faulting
    • Orogenesis: Mountain-building processes.
    • Epeirogenesis: Slow regional rising and sinking of landmasses.
  • Results: Build structural and volcanic landforms such as plateaus, rift valleys, block mountains, and volcanic peaks.

🌊 2. External (Exogenic) Processes

  • Geomorphic processes acting upon the Earth's surface.
  • Includes: weathering, mass movement, erosion, and deposition.
  • Results: Modify, roughen, and lower down pre-existing volcanic and structural features.

The present landmass of Ethiopia and the Horn is the combined effect of both endogenic and exogenic processes.

⏱️ 2.3 Geological Time Scale & Age Dating Techniques

Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago, and the earliest life appeared around 3.5 billion years ago.

⏳ Time Divisions

Geologists divide geological time based on major geological events (e.g., mass extinctions) and evolutionary changes. Time units vary in duration:

  1. Eon: The largest unit of geological time.
  2. Era: Subdivisions of eons (e.g., Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic).
  3. Period: Subdivisions of eras.
  4. Epoch: The smallest unit of time, encompassing millions of years.

🔬 Age Dating Techniques

A. Relative Dating

  • Provides comparative ages (which rock layer or fossil is older or younger) without exact numbers.
  • Method 1 (Fossil Evidence): If a rock contains fossils unique to a specific time period, the rock formed during that period.
  • Method 2 (Law of Superposition): In undisturbed sedimentary rock layers, younger layers lie on top of older ones.

B. Absolute Dating (Radiometric Techniques)

  • Uses radioactive decay of unstable elements (discovered in 1896) as natural clocks to give numerical ages.
  • Half-life: The time required for half of the original radioactive isotope to decay.
  • Carbon-14 Method: Measures $C^{14}$ decay in organic remains. Half-life is 5,730 years. Stops accumulating upon organism death.
  • Potassium-Argon ($K-Ar$) Method: Used for dating potassium-bearing minerals (mica, feldspar, hornblende) in whole rock samples.
    Note: Temperatures $>125^\circ\text{C}$ cause Argon leakage, resetting the clock to the latest heating event.

🗿 2.4 Geologic Processes & Landforms of Ethiopia and the Horn

2.4.1 The Precambrian Era (4.5 Billion – 600 Million Years Ago)

  • Covers 5/6ths of Earth's total history.
  • Limited detailed knowledge due to extreme age and lack of well-preserved fossils.
  • Major Event — Orogenesis: Intense mountain building accompanied by heavy folding and intrusive igneous activity. Created massive mountain ranges.
  • Denudation: Long subsequent periods of erosion completely leveled these mountains into near-flat surfaces called peneplains.
  • Metamorphism & Mineralization: Overlying weight, tectonic pressure, heat, and igneous intrusions transformed original sedimentary/igneous rocks into crystalline metamorphic rocks.
📍 Distribution of Precambrian Basement Rocks in Ethiopia (25% surface cover):
  • Western Part: Gambella, Benishangul-Gumuz (Metekel & Asossa), western Gojjam, western Wellega, Illuababora, Abay Gorge.
  • Southern Part: Guji, Southern Omo, parts of southern Bale and Borena.
  • Northern Part: Western lowlands, parts of northern and central Tigray.
  • Eastern Part: Eastern Hararghe.

🍂 2.4.2 The Paleozoic Era (600 – 225 Million Years Ago)

  • Lasted about 375 million years.
  • Dominant Process — Prolonged Denudation: Intense erosion worn down the Precambrian mountain ranges into peneplained surfaces with isolated residual hills (inselbergs).
  • Eroded materials were carried southward (forming continental deposits in Africa) and eastward (forming marine deposits).
  • Rock Presence: Due to continuous erosion rather than deposition within Ethiopia, rocks of Paleozoic origin are extremely rare in the country.

🌊 2.4.3 The Mesozoic Era (225 – 70 Million Years Ago)

Lasted about 155 million years. It was characterized by alternating slow sinking and rising of the landmass (epeirogenesis) and tilting of the Horn of Africa landmass.

1. Marine Transgression (Sea Invasion)

  • Began in late Triassic and continued through the Jurassic period.
  • Land tilted eastward: lowered in the southeast and raised in the northwest.
  • The shallow sea invaded from the Indian Ocean through Somalia and Ogaden, spreading northwestward toward Tigray.
  • Deposition Sequence:
    1. Adigrat Sandstone: Sands laid over the basement Precambrian rocks during initial shallow sea spreading.
    2. Hintalo Limestone: As sea depth increased, mud, shale, gypsum, and abundant marine organic remains (calcium carbonate) accumulated.

2. Marine Regression (Sea Retreat)

  • Began in Upper Jurassic and continued through the Cretaceous period due to slow uplifting of the land.
  • The sea receded back toward the southeast.
  • Deposition Sequence: Gypsum, shale, clay, and finally sands were laid over the Hintalo limestone.
    • Upper Sandstone: The top sedimentary layer deposited during the sea's retreat.
📌 Key Characteristics of Mesozoic Sedimentary Rocks:
  • Covers 25% of Ethiopia’s land surface.
  • Spatial Variation:
    • Adigrat Sandstone: Older and thicker in the SE; younger and thinner toward the NW.
    • Upper Sandstone: Thicker and younger in the SE; older and thinner toward the NW.
  • Exposures: Surface exposures occur in SE lowlands (Ogaden), central Tigray, and deep river gorges (Abay, Wabishebelle).
  • Economic Value: Greatest potential for oil, natural gas, and cement raw materials.

🌋 2.4.4 The Cenozoic Era (70 Million Years Ago – Present)

The most recent era, responsible for shaping the modern topography of Ethiopia through major tectonic and volcanic activity.

🔥 A. Uplifting & Lava Flood Outpouring

  • Massive epeirogenic uplift occurred in the Paleocene–Oligocene epochs, reaching peak heights of up to 2,000 meters above sea level during the Eocene epoch (greatest uplift in central Ethiopia).
  • Crustal fracturing released vast quantities of basaltic lava flood (Trap Series / Trappean Lava).
  • Lava covered the Mesozoic sedimentary layers, forming the high Ethiopian plateau floor ($>1,000\text{m amsl}$) and huge volcanic mountains.

⚡ B. Formation of the Ethiopian Rift Valley

  • Driven by plate tectonics (tensional forces causing parallel faulting along an up-arched swell).
  • Major faulting started in late Oligocene-Miocene; main African Rift system created during the Miocene Epoch.
  • Depression of the Afar block allowed Red Sea waters to enter (Pliocene). Later Pleistocene volcanic barriers isolated it, evaporating water to form thick saline deposits (Kobar Sink).
📏 Spatial Extent & Features of the Rift Valley:
  • Total length of Great East African Rift: ~7,200 km (5,600 km in Africa; 1,700 km in Eritrea & Ethiopia).
  • Widest section: Afar Triangle (200–300 km wide); lowest point: Kobar Sink (125 m below sea level).
  • Physiographic Effects:
    1. Divides Ethiopian Plateau into Northwestern and Southeastern Highlands.
    2. Separates Arabian Peninsula from Africa.
    3. Forms Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, and Dead Sea troughs.
    4. Creates internal lacustrine basins (Rift Valley lakes).

💥 C. Quaternary Volcanism & Aden Series

  • Recent post-rift volcanic activity during Pliocene–Pleistocene epochs, concentrated on the Rift Valley floor and south of Lake Tana ($>3,000\text{ km}^2$).
  • Known as Aden Volcanics: Freshly preserved landforms including:
    • Active volcanoes: Erta Ale (most active in Ethiopia), Dubi, Afrera.
    • Semi-dormant volcanic cones: Fantale, Boseti-Gouda (Adama), Aletu (Ziway), Chebbi (Hawassa).
    • Thermal springs, fumaroles, lava fields, and ridges.

🌧️ D. Quaternary Depositions

Alternating glacial/interglacial periods caused "Pluvial Rains" in Africa, generating high surface runoff and massive lakes.

📦 Types of Quaternary Deposits:
  • Lacustrine deposits: Former enlarged lake beds (e.g., merged Ziway-Langano-Shalla, Hawassa-Shallo, Chamo-Abaya).
  • Fluvial deposits: Alluvial sediments along river banks and floodplains.
  • Glacio-fluvial deposits: Glacial features on high peaks (Bale and Mount Kaka).
  • Aeolian deposits: Windblown sand/dust deposits in dry lowlands.
  • Coastal/Marine deposits: Evaporites and sediments along sea-invaded areas.

Cenozoic rocks cover 50% of Ethiopia's total surface area.

💎 2.5 Rock and Mineral Resources of Ethiopia

⛏️ 2.5.1 Primary Minerals & Deposits

  • Gold ($\text{Au}$): Mined for over 2,000 years.
    • Primary sources: Adola (Lega-dembi, Sakaro, Dermi-dama), Benishangul-Gumuz (Metekel).
    • Placer/Secondary deposits: Murmur, Shakiso, Awata, Dawa, Genale, Akobo, Sherkole, Didessa, Birbir.
  • Platinum ($\text{Pt}$): Active mining centered at Yubdo (Wellega). Occurrences in Delatti and Tullu Mountain.
  • Tantalum & Niobium: World-class ore reserve ($>17,000\text{ metric tons}$) at Kenticha in Adola.
  • Coal & Lignite: Found in sedimentary layers within Trap lava. Locations: Nedjo, Chilga (Gonder), Beressa Valley, Muger, Sululta, Dessie.
  • Gemstones: Amethyst, emerald, garnet, opal, sapphire, tourmaline. High quality Opal discovered in Wadla & Dalanta (North Wello).
  • Potash & Gypsum: Enormous reserves of potash in Dallol Depression (Afar). Massive gypsum deposits in Red Sea coast, Danakil, Ogaden, and Abay gorge.
  • Clay & Marble: Ceramic clay in Ambo, Adola, and Hawassa (Tabor ceramics). Marble quarried in Tigray (Mekelle/Adwa), West Hararghe (Galetti), and Benishangul-Gumuz (Dabus River).
  • Construction Stones: Basalt, granite, scoria, sandstone, and limestone for road surfacing and cement factories (Muger, Dire Dawa, Dejen, Messebo).

🧱 2.5.2 Major Greenstone Belts of Ethiopia

  1. Western & South-Western Greenstone Belt: Contains gold (Tulu-Kape, Oda-Godere, Akobo), Yubdo Platinum, base metals (Azali Akendeyu), Molybdenite, Bikilal iron deposits, and Benishangul marble.
  2. Southern Greenstone Belt (Adola Belt): Primary gold (Lega-dembi, Sakaro), Kenticha Columbite-Tantalite, and Adola nickel deposits.
  3. Northern Greenstone Belt (Tigray): Primary gold (Terakemti, Adi-Zeresenay) and base metals (Terer, Tsehafiemba).
