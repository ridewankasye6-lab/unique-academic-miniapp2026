Complete Study Notes — Active and Passive Voices

🗣 Active and Passive Voices

Active Voice: In active voice, the subject of the sentence performs the action. The structure follows the pattern: Subject + Verb + Object. This voice is direct and concise, making the sentence clearer.

📝 Example:

• The teacher (subject) explains (verb) the lesson (object).

Passive Voice: In passive voice, the action is performed on the subject, which becomes the receiver of the action. The structure follows the pattern: Object + Form of ‘to be’ + Past Participle + (by + Subject). The doer of the action is either omitted or placed at the end of the sentence.

📝 Example:

• The lesson (object) is explained (verb) by the teacher (subject).

🔍 More Examples:
  • Active: The chef cooked the meal.
  • Passive: The meal was cooked by the chef.
  • Active: They will finish the project tomorrow.
  • Passive: The project will be finished by them tomorrow.

In general, the active voice is preferred for clarity, while the passive voice is used when the focus is on the action rather than the doer, or when the doer is unknown or unimportant.

📖 Comprehensive Guide: Changing Tense from Active to Passive Voice (and Vice Versa)

⚙️ General Rules for Active to Passive Transformation:

  1. 1. Identify the subject, verb, and object in the active sentence.
  2. 2. Move the object of the active sentence to the subject position in the passive sentence.
  3. 3. Use the correct form of ‘to be’ according to the tense of the active verb.
  4. 4. Use the past participle form of the main verb.
  5. 5. Include the agent (doer of the action) with "by," but it can be omitted if unnecessary or unknown.
📌 Formula:

• Active: Subject + Verb + Object
• Passive: Object + Form of ‘to be’ + Past Participle + (by + Subject)

1. Present Simple Tense

• Active: Subject + base form of the verb + object
• Passive: Object + is/am/are + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: The company builds the bridge.
• Passive: The bridge is built by the company.

2. Present Continuous Tense

• Active: Subject + is/am/are + verb(-ing) + object
• Passive: Object + is/am/are + being + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: The chef is cooking the meal.
• Passive: The meal is being cooked by the chef.

3. Present Perfect Tense

• Active: Subject + has/have + past participle + object
• Passive: Object + has/have + been + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: They have completed the project.
• Passive: The project has been completed by them.

4. Past Simple Tense

• Active: Subject + past form of the verb + object
• Passive: Object + was/were + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: She wrote the report.
• Passive: The report was written by her.

5. Past Continuous Tense

• Active: Subject + was/were + verb(-ing) + object
• Passive: Object + was/were + being + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: They were painting the house.
• Passive: The house was being painted by them.

6. Past Perfect Tense

• Active: Subject + had + past participle + object
• Passive: Object + had + been + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: He had completed the assignment.
• Passive: The assignment had been completed by him.

7. Future Simple Tense

• Active: Subject + will + base form of the verb + object
• Passive: Object + will + be + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: She will finish the task.
• Passive: The task will be finished by her.

8. Future Continuous Tense

• Active: Subject + will be + verb(-ing) + object
• Passive: Object + will be + being + past participle + (by subject) (Note: The passive form of future continuous is rarely used.)

💡 Example:
• Active: They will be building the new mall.
• Passive: The new mall will be being built by them (Rare in use).

9. Future Perfect Tense

• Active: Subject + will have + past participle + object
• Passive: Object + will have + been + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: She will have completed the presentation by 5 p.m.
• Passive: The presentation will have been completed by 5 p.m.

10. Modal Verbs (Can, Could, May, Might, Must, Should, etc.)

• Active: Subject + modal verb + base form of verb + object
• Passive: Object + modal verb + be + past participle + (by subject)

💡 Example:
• Active: The manager can approve the request.
• Passive: The request can be approved by the manager.

🔑 Key Guidelines for Changing Active to Passive (and Vice Versa)

  • 1. Use of 'to be' verbs: The form of "to be" (is, am, are, was, were, being, been) changes depending on the tense of the active verb.
  • 2. Past participle form: Always use the past participle form of the main verb in passive voice.
  • 3. Agent (‘by someone’): In passive voice, it’s not always necessary to mention the doer. Use "by someone" only if the agent is important or needs emphasis.
  • 4. Omitting the subject: Passive sentences often omit the subject when it is obvious or unknown.
    Example: "The letter was written." (Who wrote it is not important or known.)
  • 5. Continuous and perfect tense forms: Ensure to maintain "being" for continuous tenses and "been" for perfect tenses.
  • 6. Modal verbs: When using modal verbs in the passive voice, add "be" before the past participle of the verb.

By following these rules and formulas, you can effectively convert sentences between active and passive voice across different tenses.

🧠 Additional Grammar Concepts Important for Active and Passive Voices

When learning about active and passive voices, there are several additional grammar concepts that are important to understand for mastering the topic:

1. Transitive vs. Intransitive Verbs

  • Transitive Verbs: These verbs require an object to receive the action. Only sentences with transitive verbs can be changed into the passive voice.
    • Example: He wrote a letter (transitive: "wrote" acts on "letter").
    • Passive: The letter was written by him.
  • Intransitive Verbs: These verbs do not require an object, so they cannot be changed into the passive voice.
    • Example: He sleeps (intransitive: no object).
    • Passive: Not possible.

2. Agentless Passive (Impersonal Passive)

Sometimes, the subject performing the action is not mentioned in passive sentences, especially when it is unknown, obvious, or irrelevant.

• Example: The book was stolen (agent unknown or unimportant).

This is useful in academic writing, technical reports, and formal communication to focus on the action or result rather than who performed it.

3. Focus on the Action or Result

  • Active Voice focuses on who does the action, which can make sentences more direct and dynamic.
    • Example: The chef prepared the meal.
  • Passive Voice focuses on the action or the result, which can shift attention to what happened rather than who did it.
    • Example: The meal was prepared (by the chef).

4. Passive Voice in Formal Writing

Passive voice is often used in formal or academic writing, technical reports, scientific papers, or business documents. It emphasizes the process, action, or outcome rather than the performer of the action.

• Example: The experiment was conducted (rather than "The scientists conducted the experiment").

5. Tense Agreement

When converting between active and passive voice, it's important to maintain the correct tense. For example:

  • • Present simple: They build houses → Houses are built by them.
  • • Past continuous: They were building houses → Houses were being built by them.

6. Use of Indirect Objects

In some sentences, both direct and indirect objects are present. In such cases, either object can be made the subject of the passive sentence.

• Active: She gave me a gift (me = indirect object, a gift = direct object).
• Passive 1: I was given a gift (by her) (indirect object becomes the subject).
• Passive 2: A gift was given to me (by her) (direct object becomes the subject).

7. Passive with Verbs of Perception and Reporting

Certain reporting verbs (e.g., say, believe, think, know, expect) and perception verbs (e.g., see, hear, feel) often use passive constructions.

• Active: People say that he is a genius.
• Passive: It is said that he is a genius / He is said to be a genius.

8. Get-Passives

In informal English, the verb "get" is sometimes used in place of "to be" in the passive voice. This construction is typically more casual and can imply an unexpected or accidental action.

• Example: He got injured in the game (instead of He was injured in the game).

9. Stative Passive

This refers to passive sentences where the verb describes a state or condition, not an action. It often uses ‘to be’ and is used with past participles that describe the result of an action rather than the action itself.

• Example: The door is closed (the door remains in the closed position, not describing the act of closing it).

10. Passive Infinitive and Gerund Forms

  • Passive Infinitive: The passive form of the infinitive can be used after certain verbs.
    • Example: I expect to be invited to the meeting.
  • Passive Gerund: The passive form of a gerund can also be used.
    • Example: I hate being ignored.

11. Double Object Verbs

Certain verbs can take two objects: a direct object and an indirect object (e.g., give, send, offer, show). In passive voice, either the direct or indirect object can be the subject of the passive sentence.

• Active: She gave him a gift.
• Passive 1: He was given a gift (focus on the indirect object).
• Passive 2: A gift was given to him (focus on the direct object).

⭐ Summary of Key Points to Remember

  • Only transitive verbs (those with direct objects) can be transformed into passive voice.
  • Passive sentences often leave out the subject, focusing on the action itself (agentless passive).
  • In formal writing, the passive voice is frequently used to emphasize the action rather than the person performing the action.
  • In passive sentences, the tense of the sentence remains consistent with its active counterpart.
  • Reporting verbs and perception verbs can often use passive forms to express general beliefs or opinions.

Understanding these additional grammar points will help you correctly and effectively use active and passive voice in your writing, making it more versatile and nuanced.

💡 Explanation of Verb Forms Used in the Story:

Active Verbs

  • 1. grew up (active): This is a straightforward action performed by the subject "my sister and I." It describes what they did.
  • 2. designed (active): This verb shows that "my colleagues A, B, and I" took the action of designing the experiment. The subject is performing the action.

Passive Verbs

  • 3. were educated (passive): The subject (we) is receiving the action of being educated. The focus is on the education received rather than who provided it.
  • 4. were given (passive): The subject (we) received the opportunity, and the action is being performed by someone unspecified.
  • 5. were concerned (passive): This indicates that the subject (we) felt concern; again, the action focuses on the subject receiving the feeling rather than who caused it.
  • 6. were allowed (passive): The action of allowing is performed by someone else, with the subject (cats) receiving the action.
  • 7. were selected (passive): The environments are the subject receiving the action of being chosen, not the ones doing the choosing.
  • 8. were performed (passive): The experiments are being done, with the focus on the actions taken rather than the performers.
  • 9. were installed (passive): This indicates that the panels were placed by someone, but the doer is not mentioned.
  • 10. were uniformed (passive): The observers were given uniforms; the focus is on them receiving the uniforms.
  • 11. were drawn (passive): This indicates that the observers were selected from the students, again focusing on the action received by the subject.
  • 12. had matriculated (passive): This shows that the students completed their courses before being selected, with emphasis on the action of completing.
  • 13. was developed (passive): The study was created by someone not mentioned, focusing on the outcome rather than the creator.
