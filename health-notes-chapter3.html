🧠 1. Nervous System

📌 Divisions of the Nervous System

A. Structural Divisions

Central Nervous System (CNS):

  • Brain: Located in the cranial cavity
  • Spinal cord: Located in the vertebral canal

Peripheral Nervous System (PNS):

  • Cranial nerves: 12 pairs
  • Spinal nerves: 31 pairs
  • Ganglia

B. Functional Divisions

Sensory (Afferent) Division:

  • Somatic sensory: From skin, muscles, joints
  • Visceral sensory: From internal organs

Motor (Efferent) Division:

Subdivision Function Neurotransmitter Effect
Somatic motor Voluntary control of skeletal muscles Acetylcholine (ACh) Excitatory
Autonomic motor Involuntary control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands Various See autonomic breakdown below

⚖️ Autonomic Nervous System (ANS)

Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic Divisions:

Feature Sympathetic Parasympathetic
Function "Fight or flight" "Rest and digest"
Origin Thoracolumbar (T1-L2) Craniosacral (brainstem, S2-S4)
Ganglia location Near spinal cord (sympathetic chain) Near or within target organ
Preganglionic fiber length Short Long
Postganglionic fiber length Long Short
Neurotransmitter (preganglionic) ACh (nicotinic) ACh (nicotinic)
Neurotransmitter (postganglionic) Norepinephrine (most targets) ACh (muscarinic)
Exception Sweat glands (ACh) -

🧩 Brain: Structure and Function

Major Divisions of the Brain:

A. Cerebrum (Telencephalon)

  • Largest part (83% of brain volume)
  • Divided into two hemispheres connected by the corpus callosum
  • Surface: Cerebral cortex (gray matter) with gyri (ridges) and sulci (grooves)

Lobes of Cerebrum:

Lobe Location Major Functions
Frontal Anterior Voluntary movement (primary motor cortex), executive functions, personality, speech (Broca's area)
Parietal Superior/posterior Somatosensory perception (touch, pain, temperature), spatial orientation
Temporal Lateral/inferior Hearing (auditory cortex), memory, language comprehension (Wernicke's area)
Occipital Posterior Vision (visual cortex)
Insula Deep within lateral sulcus Taste, visceral sensation, emotion
🎯 Functional Areas:
  • Primary motor cortex: Precentral gyrus - controls voluntary movement
  • Primary somatosensory cortex: Postcentral gyrus - receives sensory information
  • Broca's area: Left frontal lobe - speech production
  • Wernicke's area: Left temporal/parietal - language comprehension

B. Diencephalon

Thalamus:

  • Relay station for sensory information (except smell)
  • Filters and directs information to appropriate cortical areas

Hypothalamus:

  • Homeostasis regulator (temperature, hunger, thirst)
  • Controls pituitary gland (endocrine)
  • Circadian rhythms (suprachiasmatic nucleus)
  • Autonomic control center

Epithalamus:

  • Pineal gland (melatonin - sleep-wake cycles)
  • Habenular nuclei (emotional responses to odors)

C. Brainstem

Midbrain (Mesencephalon):

  • Superior colliculi (visual reflexes)
  • Inferior colliculi (auditory reflexes)
  • Substantia nigra (motor control - dopamine)

Pons:

  • Bridge connecting cerebellum to brainstem
  • Respiratory centers (with medulla)
  • Sleep and arousal

Medulla Oblongata:

  • Vital autonomic centers:
    • Cardiac center (heart rate)
    • Vasomotor center (blood vessel diameter)
    • Respiratory center (rhythm generation)
  • Reflex centers: Coughing, sneezing, swallowing, vomiting

D. Cerebellum

  • "Little brain" - 10% of brain mass but 50% of neurons
  • Functions:
    • Coordination of voluntary movement
    • Balance and posture
    • Motor learning
    • Cognitive functions (attention, language)

🦴 Spinal Cord

  • Length: ~45 cm
  • Extensions: Cervical and lumbar enlargements (for limb innervation)
  • Conus medullaris: Tapered end at L1-L2
  • Cauda equina: Nerve roots below conus
🔬 Cross-sectional structure:

Gray matter: Central "H" or butterfly shape (cell bodies)
  • Dorsal horn: Sensory processing
  • Ventral horn: Motor neurons
  • Lateral horn: Autonomic neurons (T1-L2)
White matter: Surrounding tracts (myelinated axons)
  • Ascending tracts: Sensory to brain
  • Descending tracts: Motor from brain

⚡ Reflexes

Reflex arc components:

  1. Receptor
  2. Sensory neuron
  3. Integration center (CNS)
  4. Motor neuron
  5. Effector

Types of Reflexes:

  • Monosynaptic: One synapse (e.g., patellar reflex)
  • Polysynaptic: Multiple interneurons (e.g., withdrawal reflex)
⚠️ Clinical reflexes:
  • Patellar (knee-jerk): Tests L2-L4
  • Achilles (ankle-jerk): Tests S1-S2
  • Babinski: Plantar stimulation → toes fan (abnormal in adults = upper motor neuron lesion)

🫁 2. Respiratory System

📌 Divisions of the Respiratory System

A. Upper Respiratory Tract

  • Nose and nasal cavity
  • Pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx)
  • Larynx

B. Lower Respiratory Tract

  • Trachea
  • Bronchi (primary, secondary, tertiary)
  • Bronchioles (terminal, respiratory)
  • Alveoli (alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs)

💨 Mechanism of Breathing (Ventilation)

Inspiration (Active Process)

  • Muscles involved:
    • Diaphragm: Contracts and flattens (main muscle)
    • External intercostals: Contract, lift rib cage up and out
    • Accessory muscles (during forced inspiration): SCM, scalenes, pectorals
  • Pressure changes:
    • Intrapleural pressure: -756 mmHg → -754 mmHg
    • Intrapulmonary pressure: 760 mmHg → 758 mmHg
    • Air flows in (down pressure gradient)

Expiration

  • Quiet expiration (Passive):
    • Diaphragm relaxes (moves upward)
    • External intercostals relax (ribs move down)
    • Elastic recoil of lungs
    • Intrapulmonary pressure: 760 mmHg → 762 mmHg → air flows out

🧬 3. Reproductive System

🔬 Gametogenesis

A. Spermatogenesis (Male)

  • Location: Seminiferous tubules of testes
  • Start: Puberty
  • Duration: ~64-72 days
  • Daily production: ~200 million sperm

Process:

Stage Cell Type Chromosome # Process
Spermatocytogenesis Spermatogonia (2n) 46 Mitosis
Primary spermatocytes (2n) 46 Enter meiosis I
Meiosis I Secondary spermatocytes (n) 23 Reduction division
Meiosis II Spermatids (n) 23 Equatorial division
Spermiogenesis Spermatozoa (n) 23 Maturation
💉 Hormonal control of Spermatogenesis:
  • Testosterone (from Leydig cells): Promotes spermatogenesis
  • FSH (from anterior pituitary): Acts on Sertoli cells
  • LH (from anterior pituitary): Stimulates testosterone production

B. Oogenesis (Female)

  • Location: Ovaries
  • Start: Fetal life
  • Duration: Decades (from fetal life to menopause)

Process:

Stage Cell Type Chromosome # Timing
Oocytogenesis Oogonia (2n) 46 Fetal life (mitosis)
Primary oocytes (2n) 46 Arrested in prophase I until puberty
Meiosis I completion Secondary oocyte (n) + polar body 23 At ovulation (triggered by LH surge)
Meiosis II completion Ovum (n) + polar body 23 Only if fertilization occurs
⭐ Key differences from spermatogenesis:
  • Meiosis I begins in fetal life, arrests, completes at ovulation
  • Meiosis II completes only after fertilization
  • Unequal cytoplasmic division (one large ovum, small polar bodies)
  • Finite number: ~1-2 million at birth, ~400 ovulated in lifetime

🚹 Male Reproductive System Structures

Structure Function
Testes Produce sperm and testosterone
Epididymis Sperm maturation and storage
Vas deferens Conducts sperm from epididymis
Seminal vesicles Produce 60% of semen (fructose, prostaglandins)
Prostate gland Produce 30% of semen (alkaline fluid, citrate)
Bulbourethral glands Produce pre-ejaculate (lubrication, neutralizes urine)
Urethra Common passage for urine and semen

🧪 Semen Composition:

  • Sperm (1-5% of volume)
  • Fructose (energy for sperm)
  • Prostaglandins (stimulate uterine contractions)
  • Fibrinogen (coagulates after ejaculation)
  • Fibrinolysin (liquefies coagulum after 15-30 min)
  • Alkaline pH (7.2-7.6) - neutralizes vaginal acidity
  • Volume: 2-5 mL per ejaculation
  • Sperm count: 20-100 million/mL

🚺 Female Reproductive System Structures

Structure Function
Ovaries Produce ova, estrogen, progesterone
Fallopian tubes Capture ovum, site of fertilization, transport to uterus
Uterus Implantation, fetal development, menstruation
Cervix Produces mucus (changes with cycle)
Vagina Birth canal, receives penis, outlet for menstrual flow
External genitalia Protection, sexual sensation

📅 Ovarian Cycle

  • Follicular Phase (Days 1-14):
    • FSH stimulates follicle growth
    • Follicle produces estrogen
    • One dominant follicle matures
  • Ovulation (Day 14):
    • Surge of LH triggers ovulation
    • Secondary oocyte released
  • Luteal Phase (Days 14-28):
    • Ruptured follicle becomes corpus luteum
    • Corpus luteum produces progesterone and estrogen
    • If no pregnancy: Corpus luteum degenerates (corpus albicans)

🔄 Uterine (Menstrual) Cycle

Phase Days Events Hormonal Control
Menstrual 1-5 Shedding of functional layer ↓ Estrogen, ↓ progesterone
Proliferative 6-14 Endometrial regeneration, proliferation Estrogen from developing follicles
Secretory 15-28 Glandular secretion, vascularization Progesterone from corpus luteum

🧬 Sex Determination

  • Sperm carries either X or Y chromosome
  • Ovum always carries X chromosome
  • XX: Female
  • XY: Male

🩸 4. Endocrine System

The endocrine system consists of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to regulate distant target organs.

⚖️ Comparison: Endocrine vs. Nervous System

Feature Endocrine Nervous
Signal Hormones (chemical) Neurotransmitters/electrical
Transmission Bloodstream Neurons
Speed Slow (seconds to days) Fast (milliseconds)
Duration Long-lasting Short-lived
Target Any cell with receptor Specific cells

🧪 Hormone Classification

By Chemical Structure

Class Examples Properties
Peptide/protein Insulin, GH, FSH, LH Water-soluble; act on surface receptors; rapid onset
Steroid Estrogen, testosterone, cortisol, aldosterone Lipid-soluble; act intracellularly; slow onset, long duration
Amine Epinephrine, thyroid hormones Derived from tyrosine; varied mechanisms
Eicosanoid Prostaglandins Local hormones (paracrine/autocrine)

By Mechanism of Action

Peptide hormones:

  • Bind to surface receptors
  • Second messenger systems (cAMP, IP₃, Ca²⁺)
  • Rapid effects

Steroid hormones:

  • Cross cell membrane
  • Bind intracellular receptors
  • Hormone-receptor complex binds DNA
  • Slow effects (gene transcription)

C. Thyroid Gland

  • Location: Anterior neck, below larynx

Hormones:

Hormone Composition Effect

  • Hypothalamus: TRH
  • Anterior pituitary: TSH
  • Thyroid: T₃/T₄ (negative feedback)

D. Parathyroid Glands

  • Location: Posterior surface of thyroid (4 glands)
  • Hormone: Parathyroid hormone (PTH)
⚡ Actions of PTH:
  1. Bone: Stimulates osteoclasts → ↑ Ca²⁺ release
  2. Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption; ↓ PO₄³⁻ reabsorption; activates vitamin D
  3. Intestine: ↑ Ca²⁺ absorption (via vitamin D)
Overall effect: ↑ Blood Ca²⁺

E. Adrenal Glands

  • Location: Superior to each kidney
  • Adrenal Cortex (80% of gland): 3 zones
  • Adrenal Medulla (20%):
    • Modified sympathetic ganglion
    • Hormones: Epinephrine (80%), Norepinephrine (20%)
    • Effects: "Fight or flight" response

👁️👂 5. Sensory Systems

👁️ The Eye (Vision)

Structure of the Eye

Three layers (tunics):

Layer Structures Function
Fibrous tunic (outer) Sclera, cornea Protection, light refraction
Vascular tunic (middle) Choroid, ciliary body, iris Blood supply, light absorption, lens shape, pupil size
Neural tunic (inner) Retina Photoreception

Detailed Structures

Cornea:

  • Transparent anterior portion
  • Avascular (gets O₂ from air)
  • Major light refraction (bending)

Sclera:

  • White of the eye
  • Protective collagenous layer

Iris:

  • Colored part of eye
  • Contains smooth muscle:
    • Circular muscles (parasympathetic): Constrict pupil (miosis)
    • Radial muscles (sympathetic): Dilate pupil (mydriasis)

Pupil: Opening in iris through which light enters

Lens:

  • Transparent, biconvex structure
  • Suspended by zonular fibers (suspensory ligaments)
  • Changes shape for accommodation (focusing)
  • Ciliary muscles: Contract for near vision (lens becomes rounder)

Retina: The retina is a thin, light-sensitive layer of neural tissue that lines the inner back of the eyeball. Acting much like the film or digital sensor in a camera, it captures incoming light and converts it into electrical signals that the brain translates into vision.

Aqueous humor:

  • Produced by ciliary body
  • Drains via canal of Schlemm
  • Maintains intraocular pressure
⚠️ Glaucoma: Impaired drainage → ↑ pressure → optic nerve damage

Visual Pathway

  1. Light → cornea → aqueous humor → lens → vitreous humor → retina
  2. Photoreceptors → bipolar cells → ganglion cells
  3. Optic nerve (CN II) → optic chiasm (partial crossing) → optic tract
  4. Lateral geniculate nucleus (thalamus) → optic radiations
  5. Primary visual cortex (occipital lobe)

👂 The Ear (Hearing and Balance)

Divisions of the Ear

A. External Ear:

  • Pinna (auricle): Collects sound waves
  • External auditory canal: Channels sound to eardrum
  • Contains ceruminous glands (ear wax)

B. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity):

  • Tympanic membrane (eardrum): Vibrates in response to sound
  • Ossicles (three small bones):
    • Malleus (hammer): Attached to eardrum
    • Incus (anvil): Between malleus and stapes
    • Stapes (stirrup): Attached to oval window
  • Eustachian tube: Connects to nasopharynx; equalizes pressure
  • Muscles: Tensor tympani, stapedius (protect from loud sounds)

C. Inner Ear (Labyrinth):

  • Bony labyrinth (filled with perilymph):
    • Cochlea: Hearing
    • Vestibule: Utricle and saccule (static balance)
    • Semicircular canals: Dynamic balance (3)
  • Membranous labyrinth (filled with endolymph):
    • Inside bony labyrinth
    • Contains sensory receptors

Hearing Mechanism

  1. Sound waves enter external auditory canal
  2. Tympanic membrane vibrates
  3. Ossicles amplify and transmit vibrations to oval window
  4. Stapes moves oval window → creates pressure waves in perilymph
  5. Waves travel through scala vestibuli → scala tympani
  6. Basilar membrane vibrates → hair cells in organ of Corti bend
  7. Hair cell depolarization → neurotransmitter release
  8. Action potentials in cochlear nerve (CN VIII)
  9. Signal to brainstem → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate → auditory cortex (temporal lobe)

👅 Taste (Gustation)

Taste Buds:

  • Location: Primarily on tongue, also soft palate, epiglottis
  • Number: ~10,000 taste buds
  • Structure: 50-100 taste cells per bud
  • Taste pores: Openings where microvilli contact saliva
  • Lifespan: Taste cells regenerate every 10-14 days

Five Basic Taste Qualities:

Taste Stimulus Mechanism Location Preference
Sweet Sugars, some amino acids G-protein (gustducin) → cAMP Tip of tongue
Salty Na⁺ ions Na⁺ channels → depolarization Tip and sides
Sour H⁺ (acids) H⁺ channels, K⁺ channel block Sides
Bitter Alkaloids, toxins G-protein → IP₃ → ↑ Ca²⁺ Back of tongue
Umami Glutamate G-protein (taste mGluR4) Throughout

👃 Olfaction (Smell)

Olfactory Epithelium:

  • Location: Superior nasal cavity
  • Area: ~2.5 cm²
  • Cell types:
    • Olfactory receptor cells: Bipolar neurons with cilia
    • Supporting cells: Sustentacular cells
    • Basal cells: Stem cells (regenerate every 30-60 days)

Olfactory Transduction:

  1. Odorant binds to receptor protein (G-protein coupled)
  2. G-protein (Golf) activates adenylyl cyclase
  3. ↑ cAMP opens cyclic nucleotide-gated channels
  4. Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ influx → depolarization
  5. Action potential in olfactory nerve (CN I)
