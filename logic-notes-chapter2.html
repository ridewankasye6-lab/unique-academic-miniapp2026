Basic Concepts of Logic

🧠 What is Logic?

🔍 Etymology & Definition

The word logic comes from the Greek word logos, which means sentence, discourse, reason, truth, and rule.

Logic can be defined in several complementary ways:

  • Broad definition: The science that evaluates arguments and the study of correct reasoning.
  • Methodological definition: The study of methods and principles used to evaluate whether premises adequately support or provide good evidence for conclusions.
  • Practical definition: A science that helps develop principles to evaluate the arguments of others and construct sound arguments of your own.

💡 Primary Aim: Logic attempts to codify and organize the rules of rational thought, creating a system of methods that serve as criteria for evaluation and guides for construction.

📚 What Do We Study in Logic?

  • 🗣️ Forms of Argument: Structural patterns that preserve truth.
  • 📜 General Principles: Core rules governing rational thought.
  • ❌ Particular Errors (Fallacies): Common mistakes made in reasoning.
  • 🛠️ Methods of Arguing: Systematized approaches to developing arguments.

💡 Benefits of Studying Logic

"Logic sharpens and refines our natural gifts to think, reason and argue." — C. S. Layman

Key advantages gained from studying logic include:

  • Argumentation Skills: Develops ability to build sound, fallacy-free arguments and evaluate claims.
  • Intellectual Defense: Serves as a fundamental defense against prejudiced and uncivilized attitudes.
  • Quality Assessment: Helps distinguish good arguments from bad ones clearly.
  • Error Identification: Enables quick detection of common logical fallacies and linguistic confusions.
  • Civic & Political Awareness: Uncovers ill-conceived political policies, exposes disguises, and separates the rational from the irrational.

🎯 Ultimate Goal: To produce individuals who are critical, rational, and reasonable in both public and private life.

⚖️ What is an Argument?

In logic, an argument is a systematic combination of two or more statements, classified into premise(s) and a conclusion.

⚠️ Logical Meaning vs. Everyday Fighting: An argument in logic is not a verbal fight or disagreement with someone. It requires a specific structural relationship between declarative statements.

📖 Essential Components of an Argument

  1. Group of Statements: Must contain two or more statements.
    Note: A statement is a declarative sentence that has a truth-value (either True or False).
  2. Division of Roles: Must contain at least one premise and one and only one conclusion.

📌 Statement Examples:

  • a. Haile G/Selase is an Ethiopian athlete. (True)
  • b. Ethiopia was colonized by Germany. (False)
  • c. Ethiopia is a landlocked country. (True)

❌ Non-Statements (Cannot form arguments):

  • ❓ Question: "Would you close the window?"
  • 💥 Exclamation: "Right on!"
  • 📢 Command: "Give me your ID card now!"
  • 💡 Suggestion: "I suggest that you read philosophy texts."
  • 🤝 Proposal: "Let us study together."

🧩 Premises vs. Conclusion

🧱 Premise: Statement offering evidence, grounds, or reasons for accepting the claim.

🎯 Conclusion: Statement that is claimed to follow from the given evidence/premises.

📌 Example Walkthrough

Example 1:

  • Premise 1: All Ethiopians are Africans.
  • Premise 2: Tsionawit is Ethiopian.
  • 👉 Conclusion: Therefore, Tsionawit is African. (Good Argument)

Example 2:

  • Premise 1: Some Africans are black.
  • Premise 2: Zelalem is an African.
  • 👉 Conclusion: Therefore, Zelalem is black. (Bad Argument - Premises fail to guarantee conclusion)

🔍 Indicator Words Technique

Indicators are clues that reveal premises and conclusions:

🎯 Conclusion Indicators 🧱 Premise Indicators
Therefore, Thus, Hence, So, Consequently, It follows that, As a result, Entails that, We may infer Since, Because, For, Given that, As, Seeing that, Owing to, For the reason that, Inasmuch as

⚠️ Special Case — "For this reason": Placed between premise and conclusion. The statement before it is the premise; the statement after it is the conclusion.

🔬 Recognizing Argumentative Passages

For a passage to purports to prove something, two essential conditions must be fulfilled:

  1. Factual Claim: At least one statement must claim to present evidence or reasons.
  2. Inferential Claim: There must be a claim that the evidence supports or implies something.

Types of Inferential Claims

  • Explicit Inferential Claim: Uses explicit indicator words (e.g., "Gemechu is my biological father, because my mother told me so.")
  • Implicit Inferential Claim: Contains an inferential link without indicator words.

🚫 Non-Argumentative Passages

  • Simple Non-inferential Passages: Warnings, advice, opinions, loosely associated statements, and reports.
  • Expository Passages: Topic sentences developed by subsequent sentences (unless the goal is explicitly to prove the topic sentence).
  • Illustrations: Examples showing how something is done or what it means.
  • Explanations: Composed of an Explanandum (phenomenon to explain) and an Explanans (statement showing why it occurred). Moves backward from fact to cause.
  • Conditional Statements: "If... then..." statements. Expresses necessary and sufficient conditions, but alone does not make an argument.

⚖️ Deductive vs. Inductive Arguments

Feature 📐 Deductive Argument 📊 Inductive Argument
Claimed Strength Premises guarantee truth of conclusion with strict necessity. Premises offer probable support for conclusion.
Indicators Certainly, necessarily, definitely, absolutely Probably, plausibly, likely, reasonable to infer
Evaluation Criteria Validity & Soundness Strength & Cogency

📐 Common Deductive Forms

  • Mathematics: Arguments based on arithmetic/geometry (excluding statistics).
  • Definition: Conclusion follows solely from the definition of a term.
  • Categorical Syllogism: Uses quantifiers like All, No, Some.
  • Hypothetical Syllogism: Contains conditional ("If... then") statements.
  • Disjunctive Syllogism: Contains an "Either... or" statement.

📊 Common Inductive Forms

  • Prediction: Premises about past/present events leading to a future claim.
  • Analogy: Argument based on similarity between two entities.
  • Inductive Generalization: Claims about a population based on sample data.
  • Argument from Authority: Conclusion based on expert testimony or witness statement.
  • Argument from Signs: Conclusion inferred from signs, symbols, or cautionary marks.
  • Causal Inference: Reasoning from cause to effect or effect to cause.

📌 Key Takeaway on Evaluation:

  • Deductive: Sound = Valid + All True Premises.
  • Inductive: Cogent = Strong + All True Premises.
