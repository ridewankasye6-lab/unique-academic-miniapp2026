🧠 Psychology

📗 Chapter 1 — Essence of Psychology

🧠 1.1 Definition of Psychology & Related Concepts

The word psychology comes from two Greek words: psyche (Ψ) meaning mind, soul, or spirit, and logos meaning study, knowledge, or discourse.

Psychology is formally defined as the scientific study of human behavior and underlying mental processes.

🔬 Science

Psychology is a science because it relies on:

  • Scientific procedures and structured experiments
  • Empirical data (observable and measurable evidence)
  • Avoiding reliance on guesswork, intuition, or common sense
💡 Example: Instead of assuming "girls are more emotional than boys" based on personal opinion, a psychologist collects objective data using standardized emotional measurement tools.

🚶 Behavior

Behavior includes all actions and reactions that can be categorized into two forms:

  • Overt Behavior: Observable actions like talking, smiling, moving, or facial expressions.
  • Covert Behavior: Unobservable internal activities like thinking, feeling, and remembering (mental processes).
💡 Example: Ever cried during a sad movie? Your tears are overt behavior, while the empathy and sadness you feel inside are covert mental processes.

❓ What Makes Psychology Unique?

  • Focus on Individuals: Unlike Sociology or Anthropology (which study group behavior and culture), psychology focuses primarily on the individual.
  • All Dimensions: Psychology covers all aspects of human behavior, rather than limiting itself to just one dimension.

🎯 1.2 The Four Goals of Psychology

Psychology seeks to fulfill four systematic goals when examining behavior:

  1. Description: Observing and detailing behavior accurately (answers "What is happening?").
  2. Explanation: Identifying causes and understanding reasons behind behavior (answers "Why is it happening?").
  3. Prediction: Determining how conditions will influence future behavior (answers "When will it happen again?").
  4. Control: Modifying or changing undesirable behavior into positive, desirable actions.

🏛️ 1.3 Historical Background

Psychology transitioned from philosophy to an independent science in 1879 when Wilhelm Wundt ("Father of Modern Psychology") established the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany.

Wundt introduced objective introspection to scientifically record conscious mental experiences.

📜 Early Schools of Psychology

1. Structuralism

  • Founder: Edward Titchener (based on Wundt's work).
  • Focus: Structure of the mind and basic elements of mental experiences (sensations, images, feelings).
  • Method: Introspection (looking inward at one's conscious experience).

2. Functionalism

  • Founder: William James.
  • Focus: How the mind functions to help people adapt to real-world environments (work, play, survival).
  • Method: Questionnaires, mental tests, and objective observations.

3. Gestalt Psychology

  • Founders: Max Wertheimer and colleagues.
  • Focus: Studying the whole mind rather than isolated parts. ("The whole is greater than the sum of its parts").

4. Behaviorism

  • Founder: John B. Watson (key contributors: B.F. Skinner, E. Thorndike).
  • Focus: Exclusively on observable, measurable behavior—rejecting hidden mental states.
  • Method: Direct observation and laboratory experiments.

5. Psychoanalysis

  • Founder: Sigmund Freud.
  • Focus: The unconscious mind, childhood conflicts, and hidden drives. Freud noted physical symptoms in hysteria patients often stemmed from unconscious emotional trauma.
  • Method: Clinical case studies, hypnosis, free association, and dream analysis.

🧩 Modern Psychological Perspectives

📋 Reference Case Study: On the playground, 6-year-old Sam pushes Sara off her bicycle and rides away on it.
Perspective Core Focus Explanation of Sam's Case
Psychodynamic Unconscious drives, childhood trauma, inner conflicts. Unconscious aggressive urges or unresolved internal conflicts.
Behavioral Environmental influences, rewards, punishments. Sam learned this behavior through prior rewards or observation ("Black Box" approach).
Humanistic Free will, personal growth, self-actualization. Sam's expression of unfulfilled personal needs or choices.
Cognitive Thinking, reasoning, problem-solving, memory. Sam's internal processing, beliefs, or interpretations of the situation.
Biological Brain structures, genetics, hormones, neurochemistry. Hormonal levels, biological predisposition, or brain activity.
Socio-cultural Cultural norms, group influences, social factors. Cultural environment or group behavior expectations.

💼 1.4 Subfields & Branches of Psychology

  • Developmental Psychology: Lifespan physical, cognitive, and social changes.
  • Personality Psychology: Individual differences, enduring personal traits.
  • Social Psychology: Group dynamics, attitudes, and social interactions.
  • Cross-cultural Psychology: Cultural variations in behavior and thought.
  • Industrial/Organizational Psychology: Applying psychological rules to modern workplaces.
  • Forensic Psychology: Applying psychological principles within legal and criminal justice systems.
  • Educational Psychology: Enhancing teaching methods and learning environments.
  • Health Psychology: Psychological aspects of physical health and medical illness prevention.
  • Clinical Psychology: Diagnosing and treating severe psychological disorders.
  • Counseling Psychology: Assisting individuals with milder life problems and emotional adjustments.

🔬 1.5 Research Methods in Psychology

Key Definitions

  • Research: Systematic acquisition of knowledge to solve problems.
  • Hypothesis: A testable statement predicting relationships between variables.
  • Theory: Integrated principles explaining observed facts.

Scientific Method Steps

  1. Define the Problem: Identify an observed occurrence needing explanation (e.g., student dropout rates).
  2. Formulate Hypothesis: Propose a testable explanation.
  3. Test Hypothesis: Collect data using suitable empirical methods.
  4. Draw Conclusions: Analyze collected data to form general principles.
  5. Report Results: Share methodologies and findings publicly for peer review.

Major Types of Research Methods

A. Descriptive Research: Records observations directly without altering conditions.

  • Naturalistic Observation: Subjects observed in natural habitats (limitations: observer bias, observer effect).
  • Case Study: In-depth examination of a single subject (limited generalizability).
  • Survey: Broad data gathering across large populations using standardized questions.

B. Correlational Research: Measures relationships between variables (Positive, Negative, or Zero).

⚠️ Note: Correlation does not equal causation. Proving a link between smoking and lung cancer does not directly demonstrate causation without controlled experimentation.

C. Experimental Research: Demonstrates cause-and-effect relationships.

  • Independent Variable (IV): The manipulated factor.
  • Dependent Variable (DV): The measured outcome.
  • Experimental Group: Receives actual treatment.
  • Control Group: Baseline group used for comparison.
