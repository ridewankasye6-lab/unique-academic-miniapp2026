 BASIC LOGICAL REASONING

🧠 Introduction to Logic

Logic is the study of reasoning—the methods and principles used to distinguish correct reasoning from incorrect reasoning. It provides the essential tools required to analyze arguments and evaluate whether conclusions follow validly or strongly from premises.

🌟 Why Logic Matters

  • 🔨 Helps construct sound arguments: Ensures your reasoning stands up to criticism.
  • 🕵️‍♂️ Enables identification of flawed reasoning: Prevents you from being misled by bad arguments.
  • 💡 Essential for critical thinking and decision-making: Guides rational thought in everyday problems.
  • ⚖️ Fundamental to legal reasoning and analysis: Helps lawyers build solid cases and judges interpret statutory provisions accurately.
  • 🗣️ Improves communication and persuasion skills: Clarifies thoughts for convincing arguments.

🏗️ Argument Structure

🧩 What is an Argument?

An argument is a set of statements where some statements (premises) are claimed to provide support, evidence, or reasons for another statement (the conclusion).

Key Components Table:

Component Definition Example
Premises Statements that provide evidence or reasons "All humans are mortal. Socrates is human."
Conclusion The statement being supported "Therefore, Socrates is mortal."
Inference The reasoning process connecting premises to conclusion The logical step from premises to conclusion

🔎 Identifying Premises and Conclusion

Indicator Words: Look for signal words in arguments to easily pinpoint premises and conclusions.

Premise Indicators 🚩 Conclusion Indicators 🎯
since, because, for, as, given that, for the reason that, inasmuch as, owing to therefore, hence, thus, so, consequently, it follows that, which shows that, accordingly
💡 Standard Examples:
• "Since it's raining (premise), the ground will be wet (conclusion)."
• "The ground is wet (premise); therefore, it must have rained (conclusion)."
🇪🇹 Ethiopian Real-Life Context:
"Since Abebe's fingerprints were found on the broken vault inside the Bank of Abyssinia in Merkato, and given that CCTV cameras show him entering after closing hours, it follows that Abebe was involved in the burglary."
• Premises: Fingerprints on vault + CCTV footage.
• Conclusion: Abebe was involved in the burglary.

🔀 Types of Arguments

A. Simple Arguments

Contains exactly one premise supporting one main conclusion.

💡 Example: "She studied hard, so she will pass the exam."
🇪🇹 Ethiopian Example: "Tigist drank unfiltered water from the river in Hawassa, so she got an upset stomach."

B. Complex Arguments

Contains multiple premises, and often includes intermediate conclusions leading to a final conclusion.

💡 Example Chain:
• Premise 1: All humans are mortal.
• Premise 2: Socrates is human.
• Intermediate Conclusion: Socrates is mortal.
• Premise 3: Mortal beings eventually cease to exist.
• Final Conclusion: Socrates will eventually cease to exist.

⚖️ Evaluating Arguments

To evaluate any argument, ask two essential questions:

  1. Are the premises true? (Factual accuracy)
  2. Does the conclusion follow logically from the premises? (Logical validity)
If Premises Are... And Reasoning Is... Argument is...
True Valid Sound ✅
True Invalid Unsound ❌
False Valid Unsound (but valid) ⚠️
False Invalid Unsound ❌

🔄 Deductive vs. Inductive Reasoning

🎯 Deductive Reasoning

Reasoning from general principles to specific conclusions. If all premises are true, the conclusion must be true.

  • Moves from: General → Specific
  • Conclusion: Follows necessarily
  • Aim: Validity (logical correctness)
  • Certainty: Provides absolute certainty (if premises are true)

A. Syllogisms

A deductive argument consisting of two premises and a conclusion.

Standard Form: Major premise (General) → Minor premise (Specific) → Conclusion.

💡 Classic Categorical Syllogism:
• Major Premise: All humans are mortal. (All A are B)
• Minor Premise: Socrates is human. (C is A)
• Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (Therefore, C is B)

B. Types of Deductive Arguments

Type Description Example Pattern
Categorical Syllogism Uses "all," "no," or "some" All A are B; C is A; so C is B
Hypothetical Syllogism Uses "if-then" statements If p then q; if q then r; so if p then r
Disjunctive Syllogism Uses "either-or" statements Either p or q; not p; so q
💡 Hypothetical Syllogism Example:
• If it rains in Addis Ababa, the roads in Bole become slippery.
• If the roads in Bole become slippery, traffic slows down.
• Therefore, if it rains in Addis Ababa, traffic slows down.
💡 Disjunctive Syllogism Example:
• Either the store manager or the security guard locked the Merkato warehouse.
• The store manager did not lock the warehouse.
• Therefore, the security guard locked the warehouse.

C. Validity and Soundness

Concept Definition Example
Valid If premises are true, conclusion must follow All A are B; C is A; so C is B
Invalid Conclusion does not necessarily follow All A are B; C is B; so C is A
Sound Valid structure + all premises actually true All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; so Socrates is mortal
Unsound Invalid structure OR has false premise(s) Valid structure + false premise OR invalid structure
⚠️ Valid but Unsound Example:
• All birds can fly. (False premise — ostriches/penguins cannot fly)
• Penguins are birds. (True premise)
• Therefore, penguins can fly. (Valid structure, but unsound because premise 1 is false)

📊 Inductive Reasoning

Reasoning from specific observations to general conclusions. Conclusions are probable, but never 100% certain.

  • Moves from: Specific → General
  • Conclusion: Follows with probability
  • Aim: Strength (degree of evidentiary support)
  • Certainty: Provides probability, not absolute certainty

A. Types of Inductive Arguments

Type Description Example
Generalization From specific cases to general rule Observed 100 white swans → all swans are white
Statistical Syllogism From general statistic to specific case 90% of AAU law students pass; Kebede is an AAU law student → Kebede will pass
Analogical Reasoning From similarity in some respects to similarity in others Drug A worked in mice; humans are similar to mice → Drug A will work in humans
Causal Inference From observed correlation to causal claim Heavy smoking is correlated with lung illness → smoking causes lung illness
Predictive Reasoning From past patterns to future events The sun rose every morning in Gondar → the sun will rise tomorrow

B. Strength of Inductive Arguments

Strength Level Aspect / Description Example
Strong Conclusion highly probable given premises 99% of observed coffee beans in Yirgacheffe are high quality → next bean is likely high quality
Weak Conclusion not well supported by premises 2 of 3 observed coffee beans were good quality → next bean is likely good quality
Cogent Strong inductive argument + all premises are true Strong structure + true factual premises

⚖️ Direct Comparison: Deductive vs. Inductive

Feature Deductive Inductive
Direction General → Specific Specific → General
Conclusion Necessarily follows Probably follows
Certainty Certain (if premises true) Probabilistic
Goal Validity Strength
Failure Mode Invalid Weak
Truth Transfer Guarantees truth of conclusion Makes conclusion plausible
Typical Use Mathematics, logic, legal rules Scientific research, everyday reasoning

🇪🇹 Examples in Legal Context

📜 Deductive Reasoning in Legal Analysis:
• Major Premise: According to Article 320 of the Ethiopian Criminal Code, all theft is punishable by imprisonment.
• Minor Premise: The court established that Defendant Chala committed theft.
• Conclusion: Defendant Chala is punishable by imprisonment under the law.
🔎 Inductive Reasoning in Legal Evidence:
• Evidence: Defendant's fingerprints found on weapon, phone signal placed him at crime scene in Hawassa, stolen items found in his vehicle.
• Inductive Conclusion: Defendant likely committed the theft beyond reasonable doubt.

🛑 Common Fallacies

🤔 What is a Fallacy?

A fallacy is an error in reasoning that renders an argument invalid, unsound, or misleading.

Fallacies are categorized into:

  • 🧩 Formal Fallacies: Errors in the logical structure of a deductive argument.
  • 🗣️ Informal Fallacies: Errors in content, context, or relevance.

📐 Formal Fallacies

Structural breakdown in deductive reasoning:

A. Affirming the Consequent

Structure: If P, then Q. | Q is true. | Therefore, P is true. (INVALID)

💡 Example: If it rains, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. Therefore, it rained.
🇪🇹 Ethiopian Example: "If traffic in Kaliti is blocked, Almaz arrives late. Almaz arrived late. Therefore, traffic in Kaliti was blocked." (False — she could have woken up late!)

B. Denying the Antecedent

Structure: If P, then Q. | P is false. | Therefore, Q is false. (INVALID)

💡 Example: If it rains, the ground gets wet. It did not rain. Therefore, the ground is not wet. (False — someone could water the garden with a hose!)

C. Undistributed Middle

Structure: All A are B. | All C are B. | Therefore, all A are C. (INVALID)

💡 Example: All dogs are mammals. All cats are mammals. Therefore, all dogs are cats.

💬 Informal Fallacies

A. Fallacies of Relevance (Premises are logically irrelevant to conclusion)

Fallacy Description Example
Ad Hominem (Abusive) Direct personal attack on opponent "Her opinion on Ethiopian tax reform is worthless because she is uneducated."
Ad Hominem (Circumstantial) Attacking based on person's circumstances "Of course he supports oil exploration—he works for a petroleum firm!"
Tu Quoque "You too" / Accusing opponent of hypocrisy "You tell me to arrive early at the office, but you are late yourself!"
Straw Man Misrepresenting argument to easily knock it down "Environmentalists want to shut down all Ethiopian factories and send us back to the Stone Age."
Appeal to Force Using threats to compel agreement "You should agree with my proposal if you want to keep your job at this firm."
Appeal to Pity Using emotion/sympathy to distract from logic "Please pass this law student; he worked so hard and his mother in Jimma is ill."
Appeal to Popularity Arguing something is true because many believe it "Millions of people believe in astrology, so it must be accurate."
Appeal to Authority Citing an unqualified or inappropriate authority "My favorite pop singer says this medicine cures typhoid, so it must work."
Appeal to Ignorance Arguing something is true because not proven false "No one has proven ghosts don't exist in that old building, so they must exist."
Red Herring Introducing irrelevant topic to divert attention "You ask about tax evasion in my shop, but what about corruption in other sectors?"

B. Fallacies of Ambiguity (Unclear or shifting language)

Fallacy Description Example
Equivocation Using the same word with double meanings "The end of a thing is its perfection; death is the end of life; therefore, death is the perfection of life."
Amphiboly Ambiguous sentence structure leading to confusion "I shot an elephant in my pajamas." (Who was wearing pajamas?)
Composition Assuming what is true of parts is true of the whole "Each player in the Ethiopian national football team is stellar, so the team is unbeatable."
Division Assuming what is true of whole is true of each part "This legal firm is prestigious; therefore, every lawyer working here is brilliant."

C. Fallacies of Presumption (Unwarranted underlying assumptions)

Fallacy Description Example
Begging the Question Circular reasoning; assuming what you try to prove "The book is completely true because it is divine." (How do you know? "The book says so.")
False Dilemma Presenting only two options when more exist "Either you buy this car right now or you don't care about your family's safety."
Hasty Generalization Drawing conclusion from insufficient sample size "I met two rude taxi drivers in Addis Ababa; everyone in the city must be rude!"
Slippery Slope Claiming one minor step leads to extreme catastrophe "If we let students use phones in class, nobody will graduate and society will collapse."
False Cause Assuming correlation equals causation "Ice cream sales increase when lake drownings rise; therefore, ice cream causes drowning."
Genetic Fallacy Judging argument strictly by its origin "This idea originated from an online blog, so it must be completely wrong."

D. Fallacies of Missing Evidence

Fallacy Description Example
Appeal to Ignorance Claiming something is true simply because not disproven "No one has proven UFOs don't exist, so they must be real."
Burden of Proof Improperly shifting burden of proof onto opponent "Prove to me that aliens don't exist in the rift valley; until then, I believe they do!"

🕵️‍♂️ Fallacy Detection Tips

  • 🎯 Identify conclusion first: Know exactly what the argument is trying to prove before evaluating evidence.
  • 🔗 Check relevance: Do the premises logically support the conclusion being claimed?
  • 🔍 Look for ambiguity: Are key terms used consistently throughout the argument?
  • ❓ Examine assumptions: What unstated premises are being taken for granted?
  • 🔀 Consider alternatives: Are other reasonable explanations or options ignored?
  • ❤️ Watch emotions: Is the argument appealing to feelings rather than facts and logic?
