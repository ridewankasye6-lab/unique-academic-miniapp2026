🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Anthropology Chapter 5

Identity, Inter-Ethnic Relations and Multiculturalism in Ethiopia

📌 5.1 Introduction & Overview

Ethnicity, race, and nationality pose one of the greatest challenges to the survival of humankind in the 21st century. They touch the very core of the social fabric (the basic structure of society), personal identity, and individuality. They influence how we think of others and ourselves, play a crucial role in our morality and political behavior, and affect our everyday existence in significant ways.

Indeed, these constructs seem to affect most things we do and think, from the most mundane (ordinary, routine) ways in which we behave to the dearest (highly valued) beliefs we hold about ourselves and others. Such identities have as much political, sociological, and economic salience (importance) today as they ever had.

🔍 5.2 Identity, Ethnicity and Race: Identification and Social Categorization

🧠 The Concept of Identity

  • All animals recognize differences between “self” and “other”.
  • In human societies, these differences take on enormous significance, partly because humans are individualistic — rather than clone-like automatons (machines), humans have unique individual personalities.
  • Society validates this individualism by giving infants unique names.
  • The notion of identity applies to the entire universe of creatures, things, and substances as well as human beings.
  • Identity is what identifies somebody or something; it is the name or essential character that identifies a person or thing.
  • The term identity is derived from the Latin word ‘Identitas’, meaning the same.
  • Identity involves two criteria of comparison between persons or things: similarity and difference.

⚖️ Criteria of Comparison in Identity

  1. Classifying things or a person: Categorizing entities based on attributes (e.g., a whiteboard, a dog, a person).
  2. Associating oneself with someone else: Group identification based on shared traits (e.g., religion, ethnicity, sex, occupation).

Fundamentally, identity is how individuals or groups define themselves and how other individuals or groups see and define them. All human identities are understood as social identities.

🌐 Sociological Definition: Identity represents the ways in which individuals and collectivities are distinguished in their relations with other individuals and collectivities.

🛠️ How Identity is Formed

Identity is formed through the socialization process and the influence of social institutions or socializing agents, including the family, educational systems, and mass media.

  • An individual is not completely free to adopt or choose any identity they like; factors like social class, ethnic group, and sex heavily influence how others perceive them.
  • The identity of individuals and groups involves both elements of personal choice and the external responses and attitudes of others.
  • Identity is our understanding of who we are and who other people are, and vice versa. It is not fixed, but rather a negotiable aspect of social life.

👥 Social Identity & Social Identification

Social Identity: Refers to those aspects of a person that are defined in terms of group memberships. Although most people belong to many different groups, only specific group affiliations are meaningful in defining self-identity.

  • Self-definition is shared with other people who claim that categorical membership (e.g., as a teacher, a Muslim, a woman).
  • Members of a given social group who identify themselves usually share features associated with the whole group.
  • Sharing a social identity with others does not mean knowing or interacting with every other member of that category, but believing that shared features exist and that group-relevant events affect individual members.

Social Identification: The process by which we define or label ourselves in terms of categories shared with other people (e.g., religious, political, or ethnic affiliations), as opposed to purely personal traits.

📖 Brubaker (2004) Perspective: Identity is not a static 'thing' that people possess. Instead, we should talk about ongoing and open-ended processes of identification. Identity does not compel people to act; rather, people actively engage in identification.

🌍 5.3 The Concept of Ethnicity

📜 Etymology & Historical Evolution

  • The term ‘ethnicity’ links back to “ethnic”, which stems from the Greek term ‘ethnos’ and Latin ‘ethnikos’, meaning “a group of people bound together by the same manners, customs or other distinctive features”.
  • In ancient Greece, ‘ethnos’ referred to non-Hellenic populations (foreigners or non-Greeks considered second-class).
  • In early English, it designated people who were neither Christian nor Jewish (heathens or pagans).
  • In its modern sense, the term was widely adopted after the end of World War II.
  • Before World War II, the term “tribe” was used for “pre-modern” societies, and “race” was applied to modern societies. Due to the association of "race" with Nazi ideology, the term "ethnic" replaced "race" in post-WWII academic discourse.
  • The term “ethnicity” first appeared in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1953.

🏢 Sociological & Anthropological Contributions

Early founding scholars of anthropology and sociology paid limited attention to ethnicity, with the notable exception of Max Weber. In “Economy and Society” (1922 / 1978), Weber offered foundational insights:

📘 According to Max Weber (1978): An ethnic group is based on a subjective belief in common descent shared by its members, extending beyond kinship, political solidarity, common customs, language, religion, values, and morality. An objective blood relationship does not need to exist; the belief in common origin is sufficient.

🌍 Regional Traditions & Global Dynamics

  • North American Tradition: Applied ‘ethnic’ as a designation for minority immigrant groups within a nation-state (e.g., Italian, Irish, Jewish communities).
  • European Tradition: Used ‘ethnic group’ as synonymous with nationhood, defined historically by territory or shared descent.
  • Post-Colonial Era: The collapse of empires in the 1950s and 1960s resulted in migration and changing group dynamics globally.
  • Global Conflicts: Out of 37 major armed conflicts in 1991, 35 were internal ethnic conflicts.
  • Ethnicity is frequently described as “vague, elusive and expansive” (Eller, 1999).

🧩 Basic Social Anthropological Model of Ethnicity

  • Ethnicity is a matter of cultural differentiation involving a dialectical interplay between similarity and difference (Jenkins, 2004).
  • Ethnicity is fundamentally based on shared cultural meanings produced during ongoing social interaction.
  • Ethnicity is not fixed or immutable; it evolves across different situations and historical conditions.
  • It operates both as collective external categorization by others and internal self-identification by individuals.

⭐ Hutchinson and Smith’s Six Features of an Ethnic Group (1996)

  1. A common proper name: Used to express the identity and essence of the group.
  2. A myth of common ancestry: Includes origins in time and place, creating a sense of assumed kinship.
  3. Shared historical memories: Recollections of a shared past, including heroes and historical events.
  4. Elements of common culture: Such as shared language, customs, or religious practices.
  5. A link with a homeland: Symbolic attachments to ancestral land, whether currently occupied or remembered in diaspora.
  6. A sense of solidarity: Present among segments of the ethnic population.

🪞 Ethnic Identity: Internal and External Aspects

Ethnic identity functions as an affiliative structure where individuals view themselves and are viewed by others as belonging to a specific cultural group.

  • External Aspects (Observable Behaviors):
    • Speaking an ethnic language
    • Practicing ethnic traditions and customs
    • Participating in ethnic institutions (religious, political, or community groups)
    • Attending ethnic events, festivals, and cultural gatherings
  • Internal Aspects (Cognitive and Affective Dimensions):
    • Cognitive Dimension: Self-image, knowledge of heritage, history, and group values.
    • Moral Dimension: Group obligations, endogamy practices, and commitment to preserving traditions.
    • Affective (Emotional) Dimension: Feelings of security, attachment, and emotional closeness to group members.

🧬 5.4 The Concept of Race & Racial Classification

📜 Historical Development of "Race"

  • Historically linked to physical and biological traits among human populations.
  • Joseph Arthur (Father of Modern Racism): Proposed three main racial categories — White (Caucasian), Black (Negroid), and Yellow (Mongoloid). He falsely asserted European cognitive superiority, ideas later weaponized in colonial ideologies, Nazi regimes, and Apartheid systems.

🔬 Modern Genetics & The Rejection of Biological Race

Modern genetic science has discarded race as a valid biological classification due to two main findings:

  1. Continuous Interbreeding: Human populations have continually migrated and interbred, preventing discrete biological boundaries (Shwartz, 2001).
  2. Genetic Variation Distribution: Genetic research demonstrates humans are > 99% genetically identical. Of the remaining < 1% variation:
    • ~85% occurs within any single local population or ethnic group.
    • ~10% exists between neighboring groups on the same continent.
    • ~5% exists between distant continental populations (Marks, 1995; Haga & Venter, 2003).

🎭 Race as a Social Construct vs. Ethnicity

  • Social Construct: Race functions as a social reality because individuals assign social meaning to physical attributes.
  • Pierre van den Berghe (1983): Viewed race as a "special marker of ethnicity" using phenotypic characteristics to delineate social boundaries.
  • Max Weber (1922): Distinguished race and ethnicity by noting that racial identity historically presumed shared biological blood ties, whereas ethnic identity relies on subjective belief in shared culture or origin.
  • Gerald Berreman (1972, 1981):
    • Race: Defined primarily by outsiders using external physical traits, often accompanied by stereotypes.
    • Ethnicity: Defined internally by the group itself based on cultural markers.

📐 5.5 Theories of Ethnicity

🔥 1. Primordialist Theory (Primordialism)

The classical perspective in anthropology (prominent until the mid-1970s). Developed by Clifford Geertz (1963, 1973), Harold Isaacs (1975), and Anthony Smith (1986).

  • Ethnicity is natural, fixed, and ascribed at birth.
  • Rooted in deep ancestral ties: lineage, language, locality, and culture.
  • Views society as a combination of distinct, permanent social groupings.
📌 Two Main Claims of Primordialism (Smith, 1986):
  1. Natural and Innate: Ethnicity is an intrinsic aspect of human existence fixed from birth.
  2. Ancient and Perennial: Ethnic communities possess deep historical continuity spanning across time.

🛠️ 2. Instrumentalist (Situational) Theory

Supported by Abner Cohen (1974), Paul Brass, Ted Gurr, Daniel Bell (1975), and Jeffrey Ross (1982).

  • Ethnicity is situationally defined and adaptively constructed.
  • It is utilized by elites and political actors to secure socio-economic or political advantages.
  • Ethnic groups act as interest groups for resource mobilization and negotiating political allocation (Ross, 1982).
  • Abner Cohen stressed ethnic organizations as functional frameworks providing group boundaries, leadership, and political strategies.

🧱 3. Social Constructivist Theory

Pioneered by Fredrik Barth (1969).

  • Ethnicity is fluid, subjective, and continually negotiated in social interaction.
  • Focuses on boundary maintenance rather than static cultural content, emphasizing social distinctions between "us" and "them".
  • Barth's Core Concept: Ethnic identities shift dynamically based on two primary social processes:
    1. Self-identification: Internal group definition.
    2. Ascription by others: External labeling by outside groups.

📊 Theoretical Comparison Summary

Theory Nature of Ethnicity Key Drivers / Basis Key Scholars

🌐 5.6 Multiculturalism & Models of Cultural Integration

📘 Definition of Multiculturalism

  • Multiculturalism describes how societies address cultural diversity at national and community levels.
  • Rooted in the core premise that diverse cultural groups can coexist peacefully.
  • Maintains that societal strength is enhanced by respecting and preserving diversity.
  • Refers to public policies designed to guarantee equitable treatment across distinct cultural traditions.

💡 Core Principles

  • Cultural diversity offers rich benefits to society.
  • Can develop through historic migration or constitutional frameworks (e.g., Canadian multicultural policy protecting language and rights).
  • Proponents highlight rights to preserve heritage; critics note the need to balance distinctiveness with overall national cohesion.

🍲 Major Theoretical Models of Cultural Integration

🔥 1. Melting Pot Theory (Assimilation Model)

  • Proposes that diverse cultural groups blend together into a single, dominant national culture.
  • Requires groups to relinquish original cultural traits and fully assimilate.
  • 🛠️ Metaphor: A smelting pot where separate metals fuse into a new alloy.
  • 📖 Historical Quote: In 1782, Crèvecoeur described America as a place where "individuals of all nations are melted into a new race".
  • ⚠️ Criticism: Reduces cultural variation and threatens indigenous or minority heritages (e.g., compulsory assimilation policies).

🥗 2. Salad Bowl Theory (Pluralism Model)

  • Advocates a pluralistic perspective.
  • Diverse groups coexist while retaining their distinct cultural identities.
  • 🥗 Metaphor: A salad bowl where ingredients remain combined yet preserve their distinct flavors and textures.
  • Promotes integration alongside cultural preservation, fostering mutual respect within a unified political state.
