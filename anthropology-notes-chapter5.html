📌 5.1 Introduction & Overview

​

Ethnicity, race, and nationality pose one of the greatest challenges to the survival of humankind in the 21st century, for they touch the very core of the social fabric (the basic structure of something), personal identity and individuality; they influence how we think of others and ourselves; they play a role in our morality (morals, principles) and political behavior; and they affect our everyday existence in significant ways.

​

Indeed, they seem to affect most things we do and think, from the most mundane (ordinary, routine) ways in which we behave to the dearest (highly valued) beliefs we hold about ourselves and others. Such identities have as much political, sociological and economic salience (very important or noticeable) as they ever had.

🔍 5.2 Identity, Ethnicity and Race: Identification and Social Categorization

​

🧠 The Concept of Identity

​
  • All animals recognize differences between “self” and “other”.
  • In human societies, these differences take on enormous significance, partly because humans are so individualistic — rather than being clone-like automatons (machines), humans have individual personalities.
  • Society validates that individualism by giving infants unique names.
  • The notion of identity applies to the entire universe of creatures, things and substances as well as human beings.
  • Identity is what identifies somebody or something. It is the name or essential character that identifies somebody or something.
  • The term identity is derived from the Latin word ‘Identitas’, meaning the same.
  • ‘Identity’ involves two criteria of comparison between persons or things: similarity and difference.
  • The basic principle of identity is connected with similarity and difference.
​

⚖️ Criteria of Comparison in Identity

​
  1. Classifying things or a person (e.g., whiteboard, a dog, black man, etc.).
  2. Associating oneself with someone else (e.g., religion, ethnicity, sex, etc.).
​

Basically, identity is how individuals or groups define themselves and how other individuals or groups see and define them. All human identities are known as social identities.

​
🌐 Sociological Definition: Identity is the ways in which individuals and collectivities are distinguished in their relations with other individuals and collectivities.
​

🛠️ How Identity is Formed

​

Identity is formed through the socialization process and the influence of social institutions or socializing agents including the family, the education system, and the mass media.

​
  • The individual is not completely free to adopt or choose any identity they like; factors like their social class, their ethnic group, and their sex are likely to influence how others see them.
  • The identity of individuals and groups involves both elements of personal choice and the responses and attitudes of others.
  • Identity is our understanding of who we are and who other people are and vice versa. It is not fixed, rather a negotiable aspect.
​

👥 Social Identity & Social Identification

​

Social Identity: Refers to those aspects of a person that are defined in terms of his/her group memberships. Although most people are members of many different groups, only some of those groups are meaningful in terms of how we define ourselves.

​
  • Our self-definition is shared with other people who also claim that categorical membership (e.g., as a man, as a Muslim, as a teacher, etc.).
  • Members of a given social group who identify themselves usually share most features associated with the whole group.
  • To share a social identity with others does not necessarily mean that we know or interact with every other member of the designated category.
  • It does mean, however, that we believe that we share numerous features with other members of the category and that, to some degree, events that are relevant to the group as a whole also have significance for the individual member.
​

Social Identification: The process by which we define or label ourselves in terms and categories that we share with other people; not our personal identity (e.g., religious such as being a Christian, political such as being a republican, ethnic, etc.).

​
📖 Brubaker (2004) Perspective: Identity more generally is not real in the sense that it is not a ‘thing’ that people can be said to have or to be. Instead, we should talk about ongoing and open-ended processes of identification. By this logic, identity does not impel (coerce) people to do anything; it is, rather, people who engage in identification.

🌍 5.3 The Concept of Ethnicity

​

📜 Etymology & Historical Evolution

​
  • The English origin of the term ‘ethnicity’ is connected to the term “ethnic” — which is derived from the Greek term ‘ethnos’, which in turn derived from the Latin word ‘ethnikos’, meaning “a group of people bound together by the same manners, customs or other distinctive features” (Vanderwerf et al., 2009).
  • In ancient Greece, ‘ethnos’ referred to a collectivity of humans living and acting together, specifically non-Hellenic people (non-Greeks, considered second-class peoples).
  • In early England, it was used to refer to someone who was neither Christian nor Jewish (a heathen or pagan).
  • In its modern sense, the term was widely adopted only after the end of World War II.
  • Before World War II, the term “tribe” was the term of choice for “pre-modern” societies and “race” was used for modern societies (Jenkins, 2001). Due to the close link between “race” and Nazi ideology, after WWII the term “ethnic” gradually replaced “race” in both North American and European traditions.
  • The term “ethnicity” first appeared in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1953.
​

🏢 Sociological & Anthropological Contributions

​

None of the founding fathers of anthropology and sociology — with the partial exception of Max Weber — granted ethnicity much attention. Max Weber, in “Economy and Society” (first published 1922 / 1978), provided early sociological definitions:

​
📘 According to Max Weber (1978): An ethnic group is based on the belief in common descent shared by its members, extending beyond kinship, political solidarity, and common customs, language, religion, values, morality, and etiquette (manners). It does not matter whether an objective blood relationship exists; it is enough that it is believed to exist.
​

🌍 Regional Traditions & Global Dynamics

​
  • North American Tradition: Adopted ‘ethnic’ as a substitute for minority groups within a larger nation-state (referring to Jews, Italians, Irish, etc.).
  • European Tradition: Opted to use ‘ethnic group’ as a synonym for nationhood, defined historically by descent or territory.
  • Post-Colonial Era: The collapse of colonial empires in the 1950s and 1960s brought immigration of visibly different post-colonial populations to former colonizer homelands.
  • Global Conflicts: Thirty-five of the thirty-seven major armed conflicts in 1991 were internal ethnic conflicts (e.g., Sri Lanka, Northern Ireland).
  • Ethnicity is described as “vague, elusive and expansive” (Eller, 1999).
​

🧩 Basic Social Anthropological Model of Ethnicity

​
  • Ethnicity is a matter of cultural differentiation involving a dialectical interplay between similarity and difference (Jenkins, 2004).
  • Ethnicity is centrally a matter of shared meanings (culture), produced and reproduced during interaction.
  • Ethnicity is not fixed or unchanging; it changes with situations and ways of life.
  • Ethnicity as identification is both collective and individual, externalized in social interaction/categorization and internalized in personal self-identification.
​

⭐ Hutchinson and Smith’s Six Features of an Ethnic Group (1996)

​
  1. A common proper name: To identify and express the “essence” of the community.
  2. A myth of common ancestry: Includes origin in time and place, giving a sense of fictive/assumed kinship.
  3. Shared historical memories: Memories of a common past, including heroes, events, and their commemoration.
  4. Elements of common culture: Normally includes religion, customs, and language.
  5. A link with a homeland: Symbolic attachment to ancestral land (not necessarily physical occupation, as in diaspora peoples).
  6. A sense of solidarity: Present among at least some sections of the ethnic population.
​

🪞 Ethnic Identity: Internal and External Aspects

​

Ethnic identity is an affiliative construct where an individual is viewed by themselves and others as belonging to a specific ethnic or cultural group.

​
  • External Aspects (Observable Behaviors):
    • Speaking an ethnic language
    • Practicing ethnic traditions
    • Being engaged in ethnic institutional organizations (church, media, politics)
    • Participating in ethnic-related events (concerts, public lectures, rallies, dances, festivals)
  • Internal Aspects (Knowledge, Ideas, Feelings):
    • Cognitive Dimension: Self-image, knowledge of group heritage/history, and group values.
    • Moral Dimension: Feelings of group obligation, attachment, endogamy (marrying within group), teaching language to children, helping group members.
    • Affective (Emotional) Dimension: Feelings of attachment, security, sympathy, and connectedness with group members versus out-groups.

🧬 5.4 The Concept of Race & Racial Classification

​

📜 Historical Development of "Race"

​
  • First related to biological differences among humans.
  • Joseph Arthur (Father of Modern Racism): Proposed three main races — White (Caucasian), Black (Negroid), and Yellow (Mongoloid). He falsely claimed whites possessed superior intelligence, morality, and will-power, while blacks were least capable. These ideas were later exploited by Nazis and Apartheid South Africa.
​

🏷️ Historical Scientific & Folk Classification Scheme

​
Racial Category Physical Characteristics Perceived Traits & Governance (Folk Notion)
Europeaeus White; muscular; long flowing hair; blue eyes Acute, inventive, gentle; governed by laws.
Americanus Reddish; erect; black, straight, thick hair; wide nostrils Obstinate, merry, free; regulated by custom.
Asiaticus Sallow (yellow); black hair; dark eyes Haughty, avaricious, severe; ruled by opinions.
Africanus Black; black frizzled hair; silky skin; flat nose; tumid lips Crafty, indolent, negligent; governed by caprice or master's will.
​

🔬 Modern Genetics & The Rejection of Biological Race

​

Modern genetics has completely abandoned race as a valid biological variable for two primary reasons:

​
  1. Continuous Interbreeding: Human populations have continually interbred, making fixed biological boundaries meaningless (Shwartz, 2001).
  2. Genetic Variation Distribution: Genetic studies show humans are > 99% genetically identical. Of the < 1% variation:
    • ~85% is found within any single population/ethnic group.
    • ~10% is between geographically close groups.
    • ~5% is between geographically distant continents (Marks, 1995; Haga & Venter, 2003).
​

🎭 Race as a Social Construct vs. Ethnicity

​
  • Social Construct: Race exists as a social/cultural reality because people act on phenotypic perceptions. Racism assumes personality is linked to hereditary traits.
  • Pierre van den Berghe (1983): Regards race as a "special marker of ethnicity" using biological/phenotypic attributes to denote in-groups from out-groups.
  • Max Weber (1922): Differentiated race and ethnicity by stating a believed blood relationship was required for racial identity but not for ethnic identity.
  • Gerald Berreman (1972, 1981):
    • Race: Defined by outsiders based on physical traits; filled with stereotypes and inaccuracies.
    • Ethnicity: Defined by the group itself based on cultural characteristics (though outsiders can also misuse ethnic categories).

📐 5.5 Theories of Ethnicity

​

🔥 1. Primordialist Theory (Primordialism)

​

The oldest model in anthropological literature (popular until mid-1970s). Systematized by Clifford Geertz (1963, 1973), and supported by Harold Isaacs (1975) and Anthony Smith (1986).

​
  • Ethnicity is natural, fixed, and ascribed at birth.
  • Based on deep, unbreakable ties: kinship, blood lineage, locality, and culture.
  • Views society as a conglomeration of distinct, permanent social groups.
​
📌 Two Main Claims of Primordialism (Smith, 1986):
  1. Natural and Innate: Ethnicity is part of who we are from birth and never changes.
  2. Ancient and Perennial: Ethnic groups have deep historical roots that have always existed across time.
​

🛠️ 2. Instrumentalist (Situational) Theory

​

Advocated by scholars like Abner Cohen (1974), Paul Brass, Ted Gurr, Daniel Bell (1975), and Jeffrey Ross (1982).

​
  • Ethnicity is situationally defined and dynamically constructed.
  • It is manipulated by political elites and leaders to achieve specific political, social, or economic advantages.
  • Ethnic groups operate as organized collective strategies for resource mobilization and pressuring the state to allocate public goods (Ross, 1982).
  • Abner Cohen emphasized ethnic groups as organizational structures providing boundary, communication, authority, decision-making, and ideological functions.
​

🧱 3. Social Constructivist Theory

​

Led by Fredrik Barth (1969).

​
  • Ethnicity is fluid, subjective, and negotiated in everyday social interaction.
  • It is not a fixed asset people possess, but a process constructed to meet everyday needs and survival.
  • Focuses on boundary maintenance and the social-psychological perception of "us" vs. "them".
  • Barth's Core Concept: Ethnic identity is an individualistic strategy where people shift boundaries or move between identities to advance economic/political interests or minimize losses. It depends on two processes:
    1. Self-identification: How the group defines itself.
    2. Ascription by others: How outside groups label and perceive them.
​

📊 Theoretical Comparison Summary

​
Theory Nature of Ethnicity Key Drivers / Basis Key Scholars
Primordialism Fixed, natural, permanent, innate. Kinship, deep ancestral ties, biology, ancient culture. Geertz, Isaacs, Smith
Instrumentalism Flexible, situational, politically manipulated. Elite competition, political & economic gain, resource access. Cohen, Brass, Gurr, Bell, Ross
Constructivism Fluid, processual, socially negotiated. Boundary maintenance, social interaction, "Us vs. Them" perception. Barth, Jenkins

🌐 5.6 Multiculturalism & Models of Cultural Integration

​

📘 Definition of Multiculturalism

​
  • Multiculturalism is the way a society deals with cultural diversity — both at the national and community levels.
  • Based on the idea that people from different cultures can coexist peacefully.
  • Assumes society is strengthened when it preserves, respects, and encourages diversity.
  • In political philosophy, it refers to how governments create policies to ensure equitable treatment of all cultures.
​

💡 Core Ideas

​
  • Diversity benefits society as a whole.
  • Can occur naturally (e.g., immigration) or legally (e.g., Canada uniting French and English cultures by law).
  • Supporters argue individuals should retain cultural traditions; critics argue it can threaten social cohesion if not balanced.
​

🍲 Major Theoretical Models of Cultural Integration

​

🔥 1. Melting Pot Theory (Assimilation Model)

​
  • Suggests that different immigrant/cultural groups blend together into one single dominant culture.
  • Individuals give up original identities and fully assimilate.
  • 🛠️ Metaphor: Like a smelting pot where iron and carbon melt together to form steel.
  • 📖 Historical Quote: In 1782, Crèvecoeur described America as a place where "individuals of all nations are melted into a new race."
  • ⚠️ Criticism: Reduces diversity to a single monoculture and destroys unique heritage (e.g., U.S. Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 forcing Indigenous assimilation).
​

🥗 2. Salad Bowl Theory (Pluralism Model)

​
  • Presents a more liberal, pluralistic view.
  • Different cultures coexist while maintaining their unique distinctiveness.
  • 🥗 Metaphor: Like a salad — various ingredients (tomatoes, lettuce, cucumbers) are combined together, but each retains its original taste, color, and texture.
  • Emphasizes integration without losing ethnic roots, promoting mutual respect and cultural preservation within a unified state.
