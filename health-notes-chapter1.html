Biology & Biochemistry: Cell Structure, Function & Enzymes

🧬 Biology & Biochemistry — CELL STRUCTURE & FUNCTION

🔍 Introduction to the Cell

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms. The study of cell structure and function is called Cell Biology.

Cells can be broadly classified into two main categories:

  • 🧫 Prokaryotic cells: Lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (e.g., bacteria).
  • 🔬 Eukaryotic cells: Possess a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (e.g., plant and animal cells).

🛡️ Cell Membrane Structure and Function

🏗️ Structure of the Cell Membrane

The cell membrane (plasma membrane) follows the Fluid Mosaic Model proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972.

🔑 Key Components:

Component Description Function
Phospholipid bilayer Two layers of phospholipids with hydrophilic heads (water-loving) facing outward and hydrophobic tails (water-fearing) facing inward Forms the basic structural barrier
Cholesterol Steroid molecules interspersed within the bilayer Maintains membrane fluidity and stability
Integral proteins Proteins embedded partially or completely through the membrane Transport, signal reception
Peripheral proteins Proteins attached to the inner or outer surface Structural support, enzyme activity
Glycoproteins Proteins with carbohydrate chains attached Cell recognition and adhesion
Glycolipids Lipids with carbohydrate chains attached Cell recognition

⚙️ Functions of the Cell Membrane

  • 🧱 Physical barrier: Separates intracellular contents from extracellular environment.
  • 🚪 Selective permeability: Controls what enters and exits the cell.
  • 📡 Cell signaling: Contains receptors for hormones and neurotransmitters.
  • 🤝 Cell adhesion: Helps cells adhere to each other.
  • 🏷️ Cell recognition: Glycoproteins and glycolipids serve as identification markers.

🔄 Membrane Transport Mechanisms

Cells must exchange materials with their environment. Transport mechanisms are classified into passive and active transport.

🅰️ Passive Transport (No Energy Required)

1. Simple Diffusion

  • Movement of molecules from an area of higher concentration to lower concentration.
  • Occurs until equilibrium is reached.
  • Factors affecting rate: Concentration gradient, temperature, molecular size, diffusion distance.
  • Examples: O₂ and CO₂ movement across membranes.

2. Facilitated Diffusion

  • Movement of molecules across membrane with the help of transport proteins.
  • No energy required.
  • Types of transport proteins:
    • 🚰 Channel proteins: Form hydrophilic pores (e.g., aquaporins for water).
    • 🔄 Carrier proteins: Change shape to transport molecules (e.g., glucose transporter).

3. Osmosis

  • Special type of diffusion involving movement of water molecules across a semi-permeable membrane.
  • Water moves from an area of higher water concentration (lower solute concentration) to lower water concentration (higher solute concentration).
💧 Tonicity Terms:
Term Definition Effect on cell
Isotonic Same solute concentration inside and outside No net water movement
Hypotonic Lower solute concentration outside Water enters cell; cell may burst (lysis)
Hypertonic Higher solute concentration outside Water leaves cell; cell shrinks (crenation)

🅱️ Active Transport (Energy Required)

1. Primary Active Transport

  • Uses ATP directly to move molecules against their concentration gradient.
  • Example: Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase)
    • Pumps 3 Na⁺ out of cell and 2 K⁺ into cell.
    • Maintains electrochemical gradient.
    • Essential for nerve impulse transmission.

2. Secondary Active Transport (Co-transport)

  • Uses energy stored in electrochemical gradients (established by primary active transport).
  • Types:
    • ➡️➡️ Symport: Both molecules move in same direction (e.g., Na⁺-glucose co-transport).
    • ➡️⬅️ Antiport: Molecules move in opposite directions (e.g., Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger).

🔀 Vesicular Transport (Bulk Transport)

Endocytosis (bringing materials into cell)

Type Description Example
Phagocytosis "Cell eating" - engulfment of large solid particles Macrophages engulfing bacteria
Pinocytosis "Cell drinking" - uptake of fluids and dissolved substances Intestinal cells absorbing nutrients
Receptor-mediated endocytosis Specific uptake of molecules after binding to receptors Cholesterol (LDL) uptake

Exocytosis (releasing materials from cell)

  • Vesicles fuse with plasma membrane and release contents outside.
  • Examples: Hormone secretion, neurotransmitter release.

🏬 Cell Organelles: Structure and Function

Eukaryotic cells contain various membrane-bound and non-membrane-bound organelles, each with specific functions.

🧠 A. Nucleus

  • Structure: Largest organelle; surrounded by double membrane called nuclear envelope with nuclear pores; contains chromatin (DNA + proteins) and nucleolus.
  • Functions:
    • Stores genetic material (DNA).
    • Controls cellular activities through gene expression.
    • Site of DNA replication and transcription (mRNA synthesis).
    • Nucleolus produces ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and assembles ribosomes.

🏗️ B. Ribosomes

  • Structure: Non-membrane-bound organelles composed of rRNA and proteins; have large and small subunits.
  • Types:
    • Free ribosomes: Suspended in cytoplasm; synthesize proteins for use inside cell.
    • Bound ribosomes: Attached to rough ER; synthesize proteins for secretion or membrane incorporation.
  • Function: Site of protein synthesis (translation).

🏭 C. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER)

  • Structure: Membrane network with ribosomes attached.
  • Functions:
    • Protein synthesis (by attached ribosomes).
    • Protein folding and modification.
    • Quality control of proteins.

Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)

  • Structure: Membrane network without ribosomes.
  • Functions:
    • Lipid synthesis (phospholipids, cholesterol, steroids).
    • Detoxification of drugs and poisons.
    • Calcium storage (in muscle cells - sarcoplasmic reticulum).
    • Carbohydrate metabolism.

📦 D. Golgi Apparatus

  • Structure: Stack of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) with cis-face (receiving end) and trans-face (shipping end).
  • Functions:
    • Modifies proteins (glycosylation, phosphorylation).
    • Sorts and packages proteins into vesicles.
    • Produces lysosomes.
    • Synthesizes certain carbohydrates.

⚡ E. Mitochondria

  • Structure: Double-membraned organelle with:
    • Outer membrane: Smooth, contains porins.
    • Inner membrane: Folded into cristae (increases surface area).
    • Matrix: Fluid-filled space containing DNA, ribosomes, and enzymes.
  • Function: Site of aerobic respiration; produces ATP (powerhouse of the cell).
  • Unique features: Has its own circular DNA and ribosomes (semi-autonomous).

🗑️ F. Lysosomes

  • Structure: Single-membrane bound vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes (acid hydrolases).
  • Functions:
    • Intracellular digestion (breakdown of macromolecules).
    • Autophagy (recycling of worn-out organelles).
    • Apoptosis (programmed cell death).

🧪 G. Peroxisomes

  • Structure: Single-membrane bound vesicles containing oxidative enzymes.
  • Functions:
    • Detoxification of harmful substances (hydrogen peroxide, alcohol).
    • Fatty acid oxidation.
    • Contains catalase enzyme that breaks down H₂O₂ to water and oxygen.

🕸️ H. Cytoskeleton

Structure: Network of protein filaments throughout cytoplasm.

Component Diameter Composition Functions
Microfilaments 7 nm Actin Cell movement, muscle contraction, cytokinesis
Intermediate filaments 10 nm Keratin, vimentin, etc. Structural support, anchor organelles
Microtubules 25 nm Tubulin (α and β) Cell shape, intracellular transport, form spindle fibers, cilia and flagella

🎯 I. Centrioles

  • Structure: Pair of cylindrical structures composed of microtubule triplets (9+0 arrangement).
  • Function: Organize microtubules during cell division; form basal bodies for cilia and flagella.

🏊 J. Cilia and Flagella

  • Structure: Hair-like extensions with microtubule core (9+2 arrangement).
  • Function: Cell movement or movement of fluid over cell surface.
  • Difference: Cilia are short and numerous; flagella are long and few (1-2 per cell).

🌱 K. Plant Cell Specific Organelles

Chloroplasts

  • Structure: Double-membraned with internal thylakoid membrane system forming grana; contains stroma.
  • Function: Site of photosynthesis.
  • Has own DNA and ribosomes.

Cell Wall

  • Structure: Rigid layer outside plasma membrane composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin.
  • Functions: Provides structural support, protection, prevents bursting in hypotonic conditions.

Vacuole (Central Vacuole)

  • Structure: Large, fluid-filled sac surrounded by tonoplast membrane.
  • Functions: Storage (water, ions, pigments, waste), maintains turgor pressure, waste disposal.

⚖️ Comparison: Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

Feature Prokaryotic Cells Eukaryotic Cells

Membrane-bound organelles Absent Present

 ENZYMES

🧪 Introduction to Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate chemical reactions without being consumed or permanently altered in the process. Most enzymes are globular proteins (some RNA molecules called ribozymes also have catalytic activity).

📜 Historical Background:
  • 1897: Eduard Buchner discovered that cell-free extracts could ferment sugar (won Nobel Prize).
  • 1926: James Sumner crystallized urease, proving enzymes are proteins.

💎 Properties of Enzymes

A. Catalytic Properties

  1. High catalytic efficiency: Enzymes can increase reaction rates by factors of 10⁶ to 10¹².
  2. Not consumed in reaction: Enzymes are regenerated at the end of reaction.
  3. Small quantities required: A single enzyme molecule can convert many substrate molecules.

B. Specificity

Enzymes show remarkable specificity for their substrates:

Type of Specificity Description Example
Absolute specificity Enzyme acts on only one substrate Urease acts only on urea
Group specificity Enzyme acts on substrates with specific functional groups Hexokinase acts on various hexose sugars
Linkage specificity Enzyme acts on specific chemical bonds Proteases act on peptide bonds
Stereochemical specificity Enzyme acts on one stereoisomer only L-amino acid oxidase acts only on L-amino acids

C. Protein Nature

  • Most enzymes are proteins (except ribozymes).
  • Sensitive to temperature and pH changes.
  • Denatured by heat, strong acids/bases, heavy metals.

D. Active Site

  • Specific region where substrate binds and catalysis occurs.
  • Usually a small pocket or cleft.
  • Has unique three-dimensional structure complementary to substrate.

E. Cofactors and Coenzymes

Many enzymes require non-protein components for activity:

Component Description Examples
Cofactor Inorganic ions Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ca²⁺
Coenzyme Organic molecules (often vitamins) NAD⁺, FAD, Coenzyme A
Prosthetic group Tightly bound coenzyme Heme in cytochrome
🧩 Key Equation:
Apoenzyme (protein part) + Cofactor/Coenzyme = Holoenzyme (active enzyme)

🔑 Mechanism of Enzyme Action

🔒 Lock and Key Model (Emil Fischer, 1894)

  • Proposes that the active site has a rigid, pre-formed shape perfectly complementary to the substrate.
  • Substrate fits into active site like a key fits into a lock.

🤝 Induced Fit Model (Daniel Koshland, 1958)

  • More widely accepted model.
  • Proposes that enzyme active site is flexible and changes shape upon substrate binding.
  • Substrate induces conformational changes in enzyme for optimal binding.
  • Explains how enzymes can act on related substrates.
🔄 Steps in Enzyme Catalysis:
  1. Substrate approaches active site
  2. Enzyme-substrate complex (ES) forms
  3. Induced fit occurs (strain on bonds)
  4. Transition state is stabilized
  5. Products formed
  6. Products released
  7. Enzyme regenerated

General Equation: E + S ⇌ ES ⇌ E + P

📈 Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

A. Substrate Concentration

  • At low [S], reaction rate increases linearly with [S] (first-order kinetics).
  • At high [S], rate reaches maximum (Vmax) (zero-order kinetics).
  • Follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics.
🎯 Key Terms:
  • Vmax: Maximum velocity when all enzyme active sites are saturated.
  • Km (Michaelis constant): Substrate concentration at half Vmax; indicates enzyme affinity for substrate (low Km = high affinity).

B. Enzyme Concentration

  • Reaction rate is directly proportional to enzyme concentration (when substrate is in excess).
  • Linear relationship: doubling [E] doubles reaction rate.

C. Temperature

Effect on enzyme activity:

  • Low temperatures: Decreased molecular motion, slow reaction.
  • Optimal temperature: Maximum activity (usually 37-40°C for human enzymes).
  • High temperatures: Denaturation (loss of three-dimensional structure).
🌡️ Temperature coefficient (Q₁₀):
Q₁₀ = Rate at (T+10°C) / Rate at T
For most enzymes, Q₁₀ ≈ 2 (rate doubles with 10°C rise until optimum).

D. pH

Effect on enzyme activity:

  • Each enzyme has an optimal pH range.
  • pH changes alter ionization of amino acid residues at active site.
  • Extreme pH causes denaturation.
Enzyme Optimal pH
Pepsin (stomach) 1.5-2.0
Salivary amylase 6.8-7.0
Trypsin (pancreas) 7.5-8.5
Alkaline phosphatase 9.0-10.0

E. Time

Reaction rate may decrease over time due to:

  • Substrate depletion
  • Product accumulation (may inhibit enzyme)
  • Enzyme denaturation

F. Presence of Inhibitors and Activators

  • Inhibitors: Decrease enzyme activity.
  • Activators: Increase enzyme activity.

🚫 Enzyme Inhibition

🅰️ Reversible Inhibition

1. Competitive Inhibition

  • Inhibitor resembles substrate structurally.
  • Binds to active site, competing with substrate.
  • Effect can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration.
  • Vmax unchanged, Km increased.
💡 Examples:
  • Malonate competes with succinate for succinate dehydrogenase.
  • Statin drugs compete with HMG-CoA reductase for cholesterol synthesis.

2. Non-competitive Inhibition

  • Inhibitor binds to site other than active site (allosteric site).
  • Causes conformational change that reduces enzyme activity.
  • Cannot be overcome by increasing substrate concentration.
  • Vmax decreased, Km unchanged.
💡 Examples:
  • Heavy metals (Pb²⁺, Hg²⁺) binding to SH groups.
  • Cyanide binding to cytochrome oxidase.

3. Uncompetitive Inhibition

  • Inhibitor binds only to enzyme-substrate complex.
  • Prevents product formation.
  • Rare in single-substrate reactions.
  • Both Vmax and Km decreased.

🅱️ Irreversible Inhibition

  • Inhibitor binds permanently to enzyme (covalent bonding).
  • Enzyme activity cannot be restored.
💡 Examples:
  • DFP (diisopropyl fluorophosphate) inhibits acetylcholinesterase.
  • Penicillin inhibits bacterial transpeptidase.
  • Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase.

📋 Enzyme Classification & Clinical Applications

🏷️ Enzyme Classification (International Union of Biochemistry)

Enzymes are classified into six main classes based on the type of reaction they catalyze:

Class Name Reaction Catalyzed Example
1 Oxidoreductases Oxidation-reduction Dehydrogenases, oxidases
2 Transferases Transfer of functional groups Kinases, transaminases
3 Hydrolases Hydrolysis reactions Lipases, proteases, amylases
4 Lyases Addition/removal of groups without hydrolysis Decarboxylases, aldolases
5 Isomerases Isomerization reactions Isomerases, mutases
6 Ligases Joining molecules with ATP Synthetases

Note: Each enzyme has a unique EC number (e.g., EC 1.1.1.1 for alcohol dehydrogenase).

⚡ Introduction to Bioenergetics

Bioenergetics is the study of energy flow through living systems. It deals with how cells transform energy through metabolic pathways to perform work.

🔑 Key Concepts: Thermodynamics in Biological Systems

1️⃣ First Law of Thermodynamics:
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed.
Cells convert chemical energy from nutrients into:
  • Mechanical work: e.g., muscle contraction 💪
  • Transport work: moving molecules across membranes 🚛
  • Chemical work: synthesis of macromolecules 🧪
2️⃣ Second Law of Thermodynamics:
Energy transformations increase entropy (disorder) in the universe.
✔ Living cells maintain order by constantly using energy from nutrients.

🌡️ Free Energy (G)

  • ΔG: Change in free energy (energy available to do work).
  • Exergonic reactions: ΔG negative; release energy; spontaneous.
  • Endergonic reactions: ΔG positive; require energy input; non-spontaneous.

🔋 ATP: The Energy Currency of the Cell

  • Structure: Adenine + Ribose + 3 Phosphates (triphosphate).
  • High-energy bonds between phosphates (~P).
  • Hydrolysis of ATP:
    ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi (ΔG = -7.3 kcal/mol or -30.5 kJ/mol)
  • ✔ ATP couples exergonic and endergonic reactions.

🫁 Cellular Respiration: Overview

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down organic molecules (mainly glucose) to release energy, which is captured in ATP.

🧪 Overall Equation:
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy (36-38 ATP)

🎯 Stages of Cellular Respiration

  1. Glycolysis: occurs in cytoplasm 🧫
  2. Pyruvate oxidation: in mitochondria 🔬
  3. Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle): in mitochondrial matrix 🔄
  4. Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation: on inner mitochondrial membrane ⚡

🧬 Glycolysis

  • Location: Cytoplasm (cytosol)
  • Oxygen requirement: Anaerobic (does not require oxygen)
  • Starting molecule: Glucose (6C)
  • End products: 2 Pyruvate (3C), 2 ATP (net), 2 NADH

⚙️ Phases of Glycolysis

1️⃣ Energy Investment Phase (Prep Phase) - Uses 2 ATP

  1. Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate (enzyme: Hexokinase/Glucokinase)
  2. Glucose-6-phosphate → Fructose-6-phosphate (enzyme: Phosphoglucose isomerase)
  3. Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (enzyme: Phosphofructokinase-1, PFK-1) — Key regulatory step
  4. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → Dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) + Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) (enzyme: Aldolase)
  5. DHAP → G3P (enzyme: Triose phosphate isomerase)

2️⃣ Energy Payoff Phase - Produces 4 ATP and 2 NADH

  1. G3P → 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (enzyme: G3P dehydrogenase) — Produces NADH
  2. 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate → 3-Phosphoglycerate (enzyme: Phosphoglycerate kinase) — Produces ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation)
  3. 3-Phosphoglycerate → 2-Phosphoglycerate (enzyme: Phosphoglycerate mutase)
  4. 2-Phosphoglycerate → Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) (enzyme: Enolase)
  5. PEP → Pyruvate (enzyme: Pyruvate kinase) — Produces ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation)
🎯 Net yield per glucose: 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 Pyruvate

🎛️ Regulation of Glycolysis

Enzyme Activated by Inhibited by
Hexokinase - Glucose-6-phosphate
Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) AMP, ADP, Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate ATP, Citrate
Pyruvate kinase Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate ATP, Alanine

🔄 Pyruvate Oxidation (Transition Reaction)

  • Location: Mitochondrial matrix 🔬
  • Requirement: Oxygen (aerobic conditions) 🫁

Pyruvate (3C) from glycolysis is converted to Acetyl CoA (2C):

Pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → Acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺
  • Enzyme complex: Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (3 enzymes, 5 cofactors)
  • Yield per glucose: 2 Acetyl CoA + 2 NADH + 2 CO₂

🔄 Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle / TCA Cycle)

  • Location: Mitochondrial matrix
  • Discoverer: Hans Krebs (1937) - Nobel Prize 1953 🏅
  • Starting molecule: Acetyl CoA (2C) combines with Oxaloacetate (4C)

⚙️ Steps of Krebs Cycle (per Acetyl CoA)

  1. Citrate formation: Acetyl CoA + Oxaloacetate → Citrate (6C)
    • Enzyme: Citrate synthase | CoA released
  2. Isocitrate formation: Citrate → Isocitrate (6C)
    • Enzyme: Aconitase
  3. First oxidative decarboxylation: Isocitrate → α-Ketoglutarate (5C)
    • Enzyme: Isocitrate dehydrogenase | Produces: NADH + H⁺, CO₂
  4. Second oxidative decarboxylation: α-Ketoglutarate → Succinyl CoA (4C)
    • Enzyme: α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex | Produces: NADH + H⁺, CO₂
  5. Substrate-level phosphorylation: Succinyl CoA → Succinate (4C)
    • Enzyme: Succinyl CoA synthetase (succinate thiokinase) | Produces: GTP (or ATP in some organisms)
  6. Succinate dehydrogenase reaction: Succinate → Fumarate (4C)
    • Enzyme: Succinate dehydrogenase (also ETC Complex II) | Produces: FADH₂
  7. Hydration: Fumarate → Malate (4C)
    • Enzyme: Fumarase
  8. Oxidation: Malate → Oxaloacetate (4C)
    • Enzyme: Malate dehydrogenase | Produces: NADH + H⁺

📊 Summary of Krebs Cycle (per glucose = 2 Acetyl CoA)

Product Yield per Acetyl CoA Total per Glucose
NADH 3 6
FADH₂ 1 2
ATP/GTP 1 2
CO₂ 2 4

⚡ Electron Transport Chain (ETC)

  • Location: Inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae)
  • Function: Accept electrons from NADH and FADH₂ and transfer them to final electron acceptor (oxygen) 🫁

🧩 Components of ETC (4 Complexes)

1. Complex I: NADH Dehydrogenase (NADH-CoQ Reductase)

  • Accepts electrons from NADH
  • Transfers electrons to Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone)
  • Pumps 4 H⁺ from matrix to intermembrane space

2. Complex II: Succinate Dehydrogenase (Succinate-CoQ Reductase)

  • Accepts electrons from FADH₂ (from succinate in Krebs cycle)
  • Transfers electrons to Coenzyme Q
  • Does NOT pump protons

3. Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone)

  • Mobile electron carrier
  • Accepts electrons from Complex I and Complex II
  • Transfers electrons to Complex III

4. Complex III: Cytochrome bc₁ Complex (CoQH₂-Cytochrome c Reductase)

  • Accepts electrons from Coenzyme Q
  • Transfers electrons to Cytochrome c
  • Pumps 4 H⁺

5. Cytochrome c

  • Mobile electron carrier (peripheral membrane protein)
  • Transfers electrons from Complex III to Complex IV

6. Complex IV: Cytochrome c Oxidase

  • Accepts electrons from Cytochrome c
  • Transfers electrons to O₂ (final electron acceptor)
  • Reduces O₂ to H₂O: ½O₂ + 2e⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O
  • Pumps 2 H⁺

🌊 Proton Gradient and Chemiosmosis

Proton pumping creates:

  • Chemical gradient: pH difference (more acidic in intermembrane space)
  • Electrical gradient: positive outside, negative inside matrix
  • Together = Proton Motive Force

⚙️ ATP Synthase (Complex V)

  • Enzyme complex that synthesizes ATP using proton motive force.
  • Structure:
    • F₀ portion: Proton channel (embedded in membrane)
    • F₁ portion: Catalytic subunit (projects into matrix)
  • Mechanism: Proton flow through F₀ causes rotation, which drives conformational changes in F₁ to synthesize ATP.
  • This process is called Chemiosmosis (Peter Mitchell, 1961 - Nobel Prize 1978 🏅).

🧪 Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation

When oxygen is absent (anaerobic conditions), cells use alternative pathways to regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.

🅰️ Lactic Acid Fermentation

  • Occurs in: Animal cells, some bacteria
  • Reaction: Pyruvate + NADH → Lactate + NAD⁺
  • Enzyme: Lactate dehydrogenase
  • Products: Lactic acid + NAD⁺ (recycled)
💡 Examples:
  • Muscle cells during intense exercise (oxygen debt) 🏃‍♂️🏼‍♂️🏼‍♂️
  • Bacteria in yogurt production (Lactobacillus) 🥛

🅱️ Alcoholic Fermentation

  • Occurs in: Yeast, some plants
  • Two steps:
    1. Pyruvate → Acetaldehyde + CO₂ (enzyme: Pyruvate decarboxylase)
    2. Acetaldehyde + NADH → Ethanol + NAD⁺ (enzyme: Alcohol dehydrogenase)
  • Products: Ethanol + CO₂ + NAD⁺ (recycled)
💡 Examples:
  • Bread making (CO₂ causes rising) 🍞
  • Beer and wine production 🍺🍷

📊 Comparison of Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Respiration

Feature Aerobic Respiration Anaerobic Respiration/Fermentation
Oxygen requirement Required Not required
Final electron acceptor Oxygen Organic molecule (pyruvate or derivative)
Location Cytoplasm + mitochondria Cytoplasm only
ATP yield ~32 ATP/glucose 2 ATP/glucose
End products CO₂ + H₂O Lactate or Ethanol + CO₂
Efficiency High (~34%) Low (~2%)

🎛️ Regulation of Cellular Respiration

🔑 Key Regulatory Points

1. Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) - Glycolysis

  • Inhibited by: High ATP, Citrate
  • Activated by: High AMP, Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate

2. Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex - Transition reaction

  • Inhibited by: High ATP, NADH, Acetyl CoA
  • Activated by: High ADP, Pyruvate

3. Isocitrate dehydrogenase - Krebs cycle

  • Inhibited by: High ATP, NADH
  • Activated by: High ADP

4. Citrate synthase - Krebs cycle

  • Inhibited by: High ATP, NADH, Succinyl CoA

🔋 Energy Charge

  • Energy charge = ([ATP] + ½[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP])
  • Range: 0 (all AMP) to 1 (all ATP)
  • ✔ When energy charge is high (0.9-1.0): Catabolic pathways inhibited
  • ✔ When energy charge is low (below 0.8): Catabolic pathways activated

🌱 PHOTOSYNTHESIS (BASICS)

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules (glucose).

🧪 Overall Equation:
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light Energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

🔑 Key Facts

  • Occurs in chloroplasts (in eukaryotes) 🍃
  • Discovered by Jan Ingenhousz (1779) - plants produce oxygen in light 📜
  • Essential for life on Earth:
    • Produces oxygen for respiration 🫁
    • Provides organic compounds for heterotrophs 🌾
    • Removes CO₂ from atmosphere 🌍

⚡ B. Electron Transport Pathways

1️⃣ Non-cyclic Photophosphorylation (Z-scheme)

Process:

  1. Light excites PSII → electron ejected from P680
  2. Electron passes to pheophytin → plastoquinone (PQ) → cytochrome b6f complex → plastocyanin (PC)
  3. Electron transport pumps protons into thylakoid lumen (creates proton gradient)
  4. Light excites PSI → electron ejected from P700
  5. Electron passes to ferredoxin (Fd) → NADP⁺ reductase
  6. Final product: NADPH formed (NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH)
  7. Electron replacement: Water splitting (photolysis)
    • 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻
Products: ATP + NADPH + O₂

2️⃣ Cyclic Photophosphorylation

Process:

  1. Only PSI is involved
  2. Electron from P700 cycles back to cytochrome b6f complex
  3. Electron returns to PSI via plastocyanin
  4. No NADPH produced
  5. No water splitting (no O₂ produced)
  6. ATP produced (via proton gradient)

Purpose: Provides extra ATP when NADPH is sufficient

Feature Non-cyclic Cyclic
Photosystems involved PSI and PSII PSI only
Products ATP, NADPH, O₂ ATP only
Water splitting Yes No
Electron source Water Cycled from PSI

🌊 C. Chemiosmosis in Photosynthesis

  • Electron transport pumps H⁺ into thylakoid lumen
  • Proton gradient drives ATP synthesis via ATP synthase (CF₀CF₁ complex)
  • Similar to mitochondrial chemiosmosis but:
    • Protons pumped INTO thylakoid lumen (not intermembrane space)
    • ATP synthase faces stroma

🔄 Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions / Dark Reactions)

  • Location: Stroma
  • Discoverer: Melvin Calvin (1940s-1950s) - Nobel Prize 1961 🏅
  • Also called: C3 cycle (first stable product is 3-carbon compound)

⚙️ Three Phases of Calvin Cycle

Phase 1: Carbon Fixation
  • CO₂ combines with Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C)
  • Enzyme: RuBisCO (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) - most abundant protein on Earth 🌍
  • Product: Unstable 6C intermediate → splits into two 3-phosphoglycerate (3PGA, 3C)
Phase 2: Reduction
  • 3PGA → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (using ATP)
  • 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate → Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) (using NADPH)
  • For every 3 CO₂ fixed: 6 G3P produced
Phase 3: Regeneration of RuBP
  • 5 G3P (15C) → 3 RuBP (15C) (using ATP)
  • 1 G3P (3C) is net gain (used to make glucose)

📊 Summary of Calvin Cycle (per 3 CO₂)

Input Amount Output Amount
CO₂ 3 G3P (net gain) 1
ATP 9 ADP 9
NADPH 6 NADP⁺ 6
💡 To make one glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): Need 6 CO₂, 18 ATP, 12 NADPH

🌾 Comparison: C3, C4, and CAM Plants

🌱 C3 Plants (e.g., rice, wheat, soybean)

  • First product: 3-phosphoglycerate (3C)
  • RuBisCO fixes CO₂ directly
  • Problem: Photorespiration (RuBisCO uses O₂ instead of CO₂ in hot, dry conditions)
  • Photorespiration: Wastes energy; produces no sugar; releases CO₂

🌽 C4 Plants (e.g., maize, sugarcane, sorghum)

  • First product: Oxaloacetate (4C)
  • Kranz anatomy: Two cell types (mesophyll and bundle sheath)
  • Mechanism:
    • Mesophyll cells: PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ into 4C compound (oxaloacetate → malate)
    • Malate transports to bundle sheath cells
    • Malate releases CO₂ for Calvin cycle
  • Advantage: Concentrates CO₂, minimizes photorespiration
  • Energy cost: Uses 2 extra ATP per CO₂

🌵 CAM Plants (e.g., cacti, succulents, pineapple)

  • Crassulacean Acid Metabolism
  • Temporal separation: Fix CO₂ at night, Calvin cycle during day
  • Mechanism:
    • Night: Stomata open; CO₂ fixed into malate (stored in vacuole) 🌙
    • Day: Stomata closed; malate releases CO₂ for Calvin cycle ☀️
  • Advantage: Conserves water (stomata open at night when cooler) 💧

🌤️ Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

A. Light Intensity ☀️
• At low light: Rate increases linearly with intensity
• At high light: Rate plateaus (limited by other factors)
• Light saturation point varies by plant type

B. Carbon Dioxide Concentration 💨
• At low CO₂: Rate increases with CO₂ concentration
• At high CO₂: Rate plateaus (saturation)

C. Temperature 🌡️
• Optimal range: 25-35°C (varies by plant type)
• Too high: Enzyme denaturation, increased photorespiration
• Too low: Reduced enzyme activity

D. Water Availability 💧
• Water stress causes stomatal closure → reduced CO₂ entry

E. Chlorophyll Content 🍃
• More chlorophyll = more light absorption (up to a point)

🌍 Significance of Photosynthesis

  1. Primary production: Base of most food chains 🌾
  2. Oxygen production: Maintains atmospheric O₂ levels 🫁
  3. Carbon dioxide fixation: Removes CO₂ from atmosphere 🌲
  4. Energy storage: Converts light energy to chemical energy 🔋
  5. Fossil fuel formation: Ancient photosynthesis created coal, oil, natural gas ⛽
  6. Building materials: Wood, fibers, etc. 🪵
