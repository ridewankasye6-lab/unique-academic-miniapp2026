Informal Fallacies & Logical Reasoning Notes

✳️ 1. Introduction to Fallacies

In ordinary language, a fallacy refers to a false or mistaken belief. However, in formal logic, a fallacy is a defect or flaw in an argument that makes it weak or invalid.

❗️ Essential Concept: A fallacy is a reasoning error that often allows a bad argument to appear sound or persuasive on the surface.

🔍 Causes of Fallacies

  • Deductive Failures: Unsoundness due to false premises or invalid structure.
  • Inductive Failures: Uncogency due to weak premise-to-conclusion support.
  • Lack of relevant evidence or misapplication of concepts.
  • Ambiguous, vague, or misleading language.

🧩 2. Formal vs. Informal Fallacies

Fallacies are broadly categorized based on where the error occurs—in the structural form or the conceptual content.

1️⃣ Formal Fallacies

Structural flaws found exclusively in deductive arguments. They can be identified purely by inspecting the logical form.

All A are B.
All C are B.
Therefore, all A are C. (Invalid Form)

2️⃣ Informal Fallacies

Logical errors that can only be detected by analyzing the actual content and context of the argument, rather than its structural pattern.

Example:
All factories are plants.
All plants contain chlorophyll.
Therefore, all factories contain chlorophyll.
(Error: Shift in word meaning/content of "plant").

⚖️ 3. Strength vs. Truth in Inductive Arguments

Truth and strength operate independently. An argument with true premises can still be inductive-weak if the premises fail to provide sufficient probability for the conclusion.

Key Rule: If all premises are true but the conclusion is probably false, the inductive argument is always weak.

📚 4. The 5 Classifications of Informal Fallacies

Informal fallacies are traditionally categorized into five main groups:

  1. Fallacies of Relevance
  2. Fallacies of Weak Induction
  3. Fallacies of Presumption
  4. Fallacies of Ambiguity
  5. Fallacies of Grammatical Analogy

🎯 5. Fallacies of Relevance

Occur when premises are logically irrelevant to the conclusion, relying instead on psychological or emotional appeal.

1. Appeal to Force (Argumentum ad Baculum)

Using explicit or implicit threats of harm to force acceptance of a conclusion.

"Agree with the manager's proposal, or your employment will be terminated."

2. Appeal to Pity (Argumentum ad Misericordiam)

Evoking sympathy to substitute for logical evidence.

"Grade my exam higher; I stayed up all night and missing this mark puts me on probation."

3. Appeal to the People (Argumentum ad Populum)

Exploiting the desire to be accepted, admired, or part of a group (Direct Mob, Bandwagon, Vanity, Snobbery).

4. Argument Against the Person (Ad Hominem)

  • Abusive: Direct verbal attack on character.
  • Circumstantial: Discrediting based on personal circumstances/interests.
  • Tu Quoque ("You Too"): Asserting the arguer is a hypocrite.

5. Fallacy of Accident

Wrongly applying a general rule to a specific case with exceptional circumstances.

6. Straw Man

Distorting an opponent's argument to make it easy to attack, then claiming the original argument was refuted.

7. Missing the Point (Ignoratio Elenchi)

Premises support one specific conclusion, but a radically different/unrelated conclusion is drawn instead.

8. Red Herring

Changing the subject to a completely different issue to divert attention away from the original argument.

🔍 6. Fallacies of Weak Induction

Occur when the connection between premises and conclusion is too weak to support the claim.

  • Appeal to Unqualified Authority: Citing an authority who lacks expertise, is biased, or is unreliable.
  • Appeal to Ignorance: Claiming something is true because it hasn't been proven false (or vice versa).
  • Hasty Generalization: Drawing a general conclusion from an unrepresentative or small sample.
  • False Cause: Assuming a causal connection that does not exist (e.g., Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc).
  • Slippery Slope: Claiming an initial step will inevitably lead to a chain reaction of catastrophic events without justification.
  • Weak Analogy: Comparing two things that are not similar enough in relevant aspects.

🎭 7. Presumption, Ambiguity & Grammatical Analogy

Presumption

  • Begging the Question: Circular reasoning assuming the truth of the premise in the conclusion.
  • Complex Question: Framing a question in a way that presumes a hidden assumption.
  • False Dilemma: Presenting two options as the only choices when others exist.
  • Suppressed Evidence: Deliberately leaving out key evidence that alters the conclusion.

Ambiguity & Grammatical Analogy

  • Equivocation: Using a word with multiple meanings inconsistently across premises.
  • Amphiboly: Misinterpretation caused by grammatical errors or ambiguous sentence structures.
  • Composition: Wrongly transferring attributes from parts to the whole structure.
  • Division: Wrongly transferring attributes from the whole structure to individual parts.
