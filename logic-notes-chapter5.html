✳️ 3.1. The Meaning of 'Fallacy'

📖 Ordinary Language Usage:

In ordinary language usage, the term ‘fallacy’ refers to a mistaken or false belief ❌.

🧠 Logician's Point of View:

However, from the logician point of view, the term fallacy refers to a defect in an argument ⚙️. Generally, fallacies can be committed because of:

  • Logical error (error in reasoning) 🧠❌
  • The creation of some illusion that makes a bad argument appear good 🎭✨

📌 Soundness & Cogency Connection:

If deductive arguments are unsound or if inductive arguments are uncogent, then they contain fallacies 🚫. This is because such kinds of arguments have one or more false premises or they contain a fallacy (or both) 🧱.

⚙️ Causes of Fallacies

Causes of fallacies, among others, include:

  • The failure to provide genuine evidences or premises for the conclusion 🚫
  • The failure to provide premises that provide good support of the premises and conclusion 🏗️
  • The failure to address the most important or relevant aspects of the issue the arguer arguing for and so forth 🎯❌

📊 The Classifications of Fallacies

Fallacies are usually divided into formal and informal 🗂️.

1️⃣ Formal Fallacies

Formal fallacies are those fallacies that arise from an error or mistake in the form or structure of an argument and they are found only in deductive arguments such as in categorical syllogisms, disjunctive syllogisms, and hypothetical syllogisms 📐.

📚 Example of Formal Fallacy:

All tigers are animals. 🐅
All mammals are animals. 🐅
👉 Therefore, all tigers are mammals. ❌

🧩 Form Analysis: Let A = tigers, B = animals, C = mammals.

All A are B.
All C are B.
👉 Therefore, all A are C.

🎯 Analysis: This argument is invalid, because the conclusion does not follow from the premises and the conclusion proves false for there is no any A which is also found in C 🚫.

2️⃣ Informal Fallacies

Informal fallacies are fallacies that can be detected only through analysis of content of the argument 🔍. Informal fallacies are logical errors in the content of the argument but not in the structure or form of the argument 📝.

📚 Example 1 (Chlorophyll):

All factories are plants. 🏭
All plants are things that contain chlorophyll. 🌿
👉 Therefore, all factories are things that contain chlorophyll. ❌

🧩 Form: All A are B. All B are C. Therefore, All A are C.

🎯 Analysis: Since this form is valid, one might conclude that the argument itself is valid. But the argument is invalid since it has true premises and false conclusion 🚫. The word “plant” is used in two different senses. In the first premise it means a building where something is manufactured 🏭, and in the second it means a life form 🌿. Hence, the argument has the following invalid form (using two letters to indicate different meanings):

All A are B.
All C are D.
👉 Therefore, All A are D.

📚 Example 2 (Bank):

All river banks are made of soil. 🌊
First National is a bank. 🏦
👉 Therefore, First National is made of soil. ❌

🎯 Analysis: Structurally looks fine, but fails because "bank" changes meaning mid-argument! 🎭

📚 Example 3 (Feather):

A feather is light. 🪶
What is light cannot be dark. 💡
👉 Therefore, a feather cannot be dark. ❌

🎯 Analysis: "Light" shifts from weight to color/luminosity. It's a content error! 🪶❌

💡 Key Distinction: Formal fallacies are always invalid; however informal fallacies can be valid. But their validity is not genuine and logical 🎭. The correctness of reasoning in informal fallacies is only from psychological and rhetoric sense of the argument. Therefore, the effect of an informal fallacy is to make a bad argument appear good ✨.

⚠️ Important Distinctions

Aspect Formal Fallacies 📐 Informal Fallacies 📝
Based on Structure of the argument 🏗️ Content and meaning 🔍
Type Found in deductive reasoning 📐 Found in both deductive & inductive 🧠
Detection Easy to spot by checking the form Checking Requires analysis of language & context 🗣️
Validity Always invalid ❌ May appear valid, but not logically sound 🎭
Effect Conclusion doesn't follow from premises 🚫 Appears convincing, but logically flawed ✨

📌 Major Causes of Informal Fallacies

  • When the premise becomes irrelevant to the conclusion (see fallacies of relevance) 🎯❌
  • When the premise becomes unacceptable to the claims of the conclusion (see fallacies of presumption) 🛑
  • When the premise becomes insufficient to provide evidence (see fallacies of weak induction) 📊❌
  • When the premise is expressed by unclear language (see fallacies of ambiguity and grammatical analogy) 🗣️💬

🎭 Characteristics of Informal Fallacies

  • They are frequently backed by some motive on the part of the arguer to deceive the reader or listener 😈
  • The arguer may not have sufficient evidence and attempts to win acceptance by restoring to a trick 🃏
  • Sometimes the trick fools even the arguer into thinking they present genuine evidence when they are not 🤦‍♂️

1. 🎯 Fallacies of Relevance

They are fallacies that fail to provide relevant and acceptable premises to their conclusion 🚫. They provide irrelevant premises to the conclusion. The premises are relevant psychologically and the connection between premises and conclusion is emotional, not logical 💖.

📋 Fallacies of Relevance contain eight (8) different types:

  • Appeal to Force 👊
  • Appeal to Pity 🥺
  • Appeal to People 📣
  • Argument Against the Person 👤➡️❌
  • Straw Man 🌾👨
  • Red Herring 🐟
  • Accident 🚦
  • Missing the Point 🎯❌

👊 1. Appeal to Force (Argumentum ad Baculum: Appeal to the Stick)

It occurs whenever an arguer creates a conclusion to another person and tells the person either implicitly or explicitly that some harm will come to him or her if he or she does not accept the conclusion 💥. In other words, an appeal to force fallacy occurs whenever one irrelevantly appeals to force or threat of force to win an argument 🗡️.

This fallacy always involves a threat by the arguer to the physical or psychological wellbeing of the listener or reader 😨. Premises of an argument are full of threat, intimidation, scary words, etc. 🗣️

📚 Example 1 (ETV Show):

‘‘Meet ETV’’ is the best show on ETV; and if you do not believe it, I am going to call my big brother over here and he is going to beat you up. 👊❌

📚 Example 2 (Government Authority):

Anyone who believes the government has exceeded its proper authority under the constitution will be subjected to severe harassment by the provincial police 🚓. Therefore, the government has not exceeded its authority. ❌

📚 Example 3 (Teacher to Student):

A teacher to his student: Aristotle has the only correct philosophical view on this matter 📜. If you do not think so, wait to see what mark I give you on the final exam. 📝❌

🎯 Analysis: These arguments fail to provide logical evidence to the truth of their conclusion. Instead they provide a kind of harm or threat as a reason to accept their conclusion. The first two examples involve a physical threat 👊 whereas the last example involves a psychological threat 🧠.

🥺 2. Appeal to Pity (Argumentum ad Misericordiam)

It occurs when an arguer attempts to support a conclusion by simply evoking pity from the reader or listener in an effort to get him or her to accept the conclusion 😭. The pity does not have any logical connection or relevance to the conclusion 🚫. But it is psychologically relevant as the arguer can usually succeed in getting a pitying heart from his audience ❤️.

The appeal to pity is quite common and frequently appears in schools 🏫, courtrooms ⚖️, streets 🛣️, offices 🏢, etc.

📚 Example 1 (Student Grade):

A student to his instructor: Professor, this paper deserves at least a ‘B’ grade 📝. I stayed up all night working on it 🥱. And if I do not get a ‘B’, I will be on academic probation. 😭❌

🎯 Analysis: The student tries to support "deserves a B" using pitiable ideas like staying up all night and probation. It is psychologically relevant but logically irrelevant 🚫.

📚 Example 2 (Courtroom Case):

Your honor, it is true that I killed my parents 🔪. I fully admit that I murdered them in cold blood. But I should get a light sentence. After all, I am an orphan. 🥺❌

🎯 Analysis: Being an orphan resulting from one's own crime is not a logical defense against murder! ⚖️❌

📚 Example 3 (Traffic Ticket):

Officer, please don't give me a speeding ticket 🚓. I was speeding because I just lost my job today, and my dog is very sick at home 🐕.

🎯 Analysis: Personal sadness does not change the law or driving speed! 🚗❌

📣 3. Appeal to People (Argumentum ad Populum)

It occurs when the arguer attempts to persuade the reader or listener about a certain issue on the ground that most people approve it or disapprove the issue in question 👥. It consists of arguments with language calculated to excite enthusiasm, excitement, anger, or hate 🔥.

🎯 Two Approaches:

  • Direct Approach: Occurs when an arguer, addressing a large group of people, excites the emotions and enthusiasm of the crowd to win acceptance for his/her conclusion 🎤. Objective: arouse a mob mentality 🗣️. Used by propagandists, demagogues, preachers, and advertisers 📢.
  • Indirect Approach: Appeals to one or more individuals separately, focusing upon some aspect of their relationship to the crowd 👤. Used heavily in the advertising industry 📺.

Three Varieties of Indirect Approach:

A. 📈 Appeal to Bandwagon:

Emphasizes that the majority choice is the correct one 👥. Fallacious because peer pressure urges acceptance on grounds of approval of friends or associates 🤝.

📚 Bandwagon Example 1:

Chewing chat cannot be all wrong because 70% of Werabe University students see nothing wrong with it. 🍃❌

🎯 Analysis: The percentage is irrelevant to whether chewing chat is actually good or bad. Popularity ≠ Truth 🚫.

📚 Bandwagon Example 2:

A film is good because there are long lines of people waiting to see it. 🎟️🎬

🎯 Analysis: A long line shows interest, but says nothing about the actual logical quality of the movie 🍿❌.

📚 Bandwagon Example 3:

Everyone in our school is getting the new smartphone model 📱! You should get it too so you aren't left out!

🎯 Analysis: Buying a phone because everyone else has it relies on fear of isolation, not phone utility 📱❌.

B. ✨ Appeal to Vanity:

Associates the product with certain celebrities such as artists 🎨, athletes 🏃, footballers ⚽, respected leaders, etc., informing audiences that using the item will make them admired ⭐.

📚 Vanity Example 1:

Who is going to wear this new fashion T-shirt worn by the famous artist Gosaye for the new Ethiopian Millennium? 👕✨

📚 Vanity Example 2:

Who is going to buy this new fashion shoe, a shoe used by the famous Haile G/Sellassie in the London Marathon? 👟🏃

📚 Vanity Example 3:

Drink this energizing drink—endorsed by world champion football stars! ⚽🥤

C. 👑 Appeal to Snobbery:

An appeal to the desire to be regarded as superior to others 💎. Occurs when an arguer associates a product with a selected few persons (distinguished persons) who have an exaggerated social position or quality 👑.

📚 Snobbery Example 1:

This is not for ordinary people. If you want to be from among the selected few dignitaries, buy the shoe. 👟👑

📚 Snobbery Example 2:

Look at the mark of this cell phone—it is Nokia and Nokia is not for everyone. Buy Nokia and join the selected few. 📱💎

📚 Snobbery Example 3:

First of all, did you see the mark of the shoe—it's Clark? You should know that Clark is not for ordinary citizens. Buy Clark and join with the dignitaries. 👞⚜️

🚫 4. Argument Against the Person (Argumentum ad Hominem)

This fallacy always involves two arguers 👥. One commits this fallacy if someone refuses to consider their opponent's argument on its merit alone, and instead attacks the opponent on the ground of their belief, motive, religion, character, or practice 🛑.

4.1 🗣️ Ad Hominem Abusive

The second person responds by verbally abusing the first person and discrediting their character, intelligence, or reasonableness 🤬. A person can be abused for being ugly, a smoker, gambler, conservative, etc. But character is logically irrelevant to the truth of what they say 🚫.

📚 Abusive Example 1:

How can a stingy person tell us about charity 💰? Hence, let us stop discussing this issue raised by Tamirat. ❌

📚 Abusive Example 2:

Don't listen to Dr. Bekele's theory on environmental protection 🌿. He is an unkempt, rude person who dresses poorly. 👔❌

📚 Abusive Example 3:

Sara's plan for student union elections is completely foolish because she is just a lazy student who failed maths last term. 📚❌

4.2 📑 Ad Hominem Circumstantial

Instead of verbal abuse, the respondent attempts to discredit the argument by pointing to circumstances that affect the opponent (e.g., religion, political affiliation, ethnic background, job position) 🏢. Form: "Of course Mr. X argues this way; just look at the circumstances affecting him."

📚 Circumstantial Example 1:

Dr. Tewodros advocates a policy of increasing financial spending for higher education 🎓. But that is not innocent advocacy, for the reason that he is a college professor and would benefit financially from such a policy. 💰❌

📚 Circumstantial Example 2:

Of course the tax consultant argues that taxes are too complicated 📜! If tax laws were simple, he would lose his job. 💼❌

📚 Circumstantial Example 3:

The landlord argues that rent control is harmful to the town 🏠. Naturally he says so, because he owns five apartment buildings! 🏢❌

4.3 🔄 Tu Quoque ("You Too")

Pronounced "too kwo-kway". The second arguer attempts to make the first appear hypocritical or arguing in bad faith 🎭. "How dare you argue that I should stop doing X, when you do (or have done) X yourself!"

📚 Tu Quoque Example 1:

Child to parent: Your argument that I should stop stealing candy from the corner store is no good 🍬. You told me yourself just a week ago that you, too, stole candy when you were a kid. 👦❌

📚 Tu Quoque Example 2:

My doctor told me to lose some weight ⚖️. Why should I listen to a doctor who is himself overweight? 🩺❌

📚 Tu Quoque Example 3:

You tell me not to smoke cigarettes because it's bad for my lungs 🚬, but you smoke a pack a day yourself! So your argument is invalid. 🚭❌

💡 Exception — Discrediting a Witness (Not a Fallacy!):

Personal factors ARE relevant when evaluating unsupported testimony or witness claims ⚖️.

"Geremew testified he saw Belay set fire to the building 🔥. But Geremew was recently convicted on ten counts of perjury, and he hates Belay with a passion. Therefore, you should not believe Geremew’s testimony." ✔️

🎯 Note: Testimony is NOT an argument. Rejecting a liar's unsupported testimony is logically justified! ⚖️✔️

🚦 5. Fallacy of Accident

Committed when a general rule is applied to a specific case that was not intended to cover 🎯. The general rule is cited in the premises and wrongly applied to a specific case with "accidental" or atypical features 🛑.

📚 Example 1 (Freedom of Speech):

Freedom of speech is a constitutionally guaranteed right 📜. Therefore, Abebe should not be arrested for his speech that inspired the riot last week. 📢❌

🎯 Analysis: General rule: Freedom of speech ✔️. Specific case: Inciting riots ❌. The rule is misapplied!

📚 Example 2 (Obeying Doctors):

Kidist! All good patients obey the order of their doctors 🩺. Hence, you should not refuse when your doctor invites you for bed. 🛌❌

📚 Example 3 (Cutting People with Knives):

Cutting people with knives is a crime 🔪. Surgeons cut people with knives during surgery 🏥. Therefore, surgeons are criminals. ❌

🎯 Analysis: Medical surgery is an exception to the general rule against cutting people! 🏥✔️

🌾👨 6. Straw Man Fallacy (Attacking a Distorted Version)

Committed when an arguer distorts an opponent’s argument for the purpose of more easily attacking it, demolishes the distorted argument, and then concludes that the real argument has been demolished 🎯💥.

⚙️ Three Essential Components:

  • 1. Pair of arguers in dialogue 👥
  • 2. Arguing with each other 🗣️
  • 3. Advocating opposing positions ⚔️

📚 Example 1 (Beer Ads):

Mengesha: It would be a good idea to ban advertising beer and wine on radio and television 📺. These ads encourage teenagers to drink with disastrous consequences.
Tsegaye: You cannot get people to give up drinking; they have been doing it for thousands of years. 🍺❌

🎯 Analysis: Mengesha argued to ban ads 📺. Tsegaye distorted it to banning drinking entirely 🍷❌.

📚 Example 2 (Terrorist Rights):

Mary: We must not betray the principles of justice and democracy ⚖️. Suspected terrorists must be granted basic rights as well as legal representation and access to a fair court.
Tom: Mary is advocating the release of known terrorists 🔓! We cannot afford to allow our enemies to move freely in our society. ❌

🎯 Analysis: Mary advocated fair legal trials ⚖️. Tom distorted it to mean releasing terrorists onto the streets 🔓❌.

📚 Example 3 (School Budget):

Teacher: We need to allocate a bit more budget toward our school art class 🎨.
Parent: Why do you hate mathematics and science so much that you want to drain all money from them 🔬📊?❌

🎯 Analysis: Supporting art doesn't mean hating science! It's a classic straw man distortion 🌾👨.

🚫 7. The Fallacy of Missing the Point (Ignoratio Elenchi)

📖 Latin meaning: "Ignorance of the proof"

This fallacy occurs when the premises of an argument support one particular conclusion, but then a different, unrelated, or vaguely connected conclusion is drawn.

🧠 In short: the argument misses the logical point of its own reasoning.

🔍 Key Features:

  • The premises are valid and suggest a reasonable conclusion ✔️
  • But the actual conclusion drawn is off-topic, too extreme, or unrelated ❌
  • The arguer ignores the logical implications of their own argument ⚙️

📚 Examples:

📌 Example 1:

"Crimes of theft and robbery have been increasing at an alarming rate lately.
👉 Conclusion: We must reinstate the death penalty immediately."

🎯 Analysis:

  • The premise is valid: crime is increasing ✔️
  • But the conclusion — death penalty for theft and robbery — is not logically justified by the premises ❌
  • At least two correct conclusions are implied: "we should provide increased police protection in vulnerable neighborhoods" or "we should initiate programs to eliminate the cause of the crimes."
  • The punishment for theft and robbery should be serious, but it does not support reinstating the death penalty at all.

📌 Example 2:

"KKC University has a lot of problems. Students’ services and facilities are inadequate. Many of the instructors are inexperienced.
👉 Conclusion: It follows that, the university should be entirely closed."

🎯 Analysis:

  • The conclusion misses the logical implication from the premise ❌
  • The logical conclusion is not closing the university, but taking corrective actions such as: providing additional facilities for students, getting experienced instructors from other countries, developing the capacity of the administration, and the like ✔️

🎭 8. Red Herring Fallacy (Off the Truck Fallacy)

This fallacy happens when the arguer intentionally diverts attention from the main issue by introducing a different, unrelated topic — often with emotion, humor, or a provocative remark.

🎏 It's a classic distraction technique — changing the subject to win the argument or avoid answering directly.

🔍 Key Features:

  • Topic is changed midway through the discussion 🔄
  • The new topic is irrelevant, though it may appear subtly related ❌
  • It shifts the audience's attention away from the original issue 🎯

📚 Examples:

📌 Example 1:

The Minister: "The new education policy is appreciative."
Bezawit: "Did you hear about his first son? The important question confronting this great nation is the question of terrorism. Let me tell you how I plan to defeat it. He is going to marry an orphanage girl. Before the minister is talking about practical education policy, he should give a lesson for his son to get a good wife. So, his new education policy is not appreciative."

🎯 Analysis:

  • The discussion starts on education policy ✔️
  • The arguer diverts the topic to the minister's son marrying an orphan girl ❌
  • Personal marriage choices are completely irrelevant to evaluating the educational policy.

📌 Example 2:

Interviewer: "Your opponent has argued for immigration reform. Do you agree with her position?"
Candidate: "I think the more important question confronting this great nation is the question of terrorism. Let me tell you how I plan to defeat it."

🎯 Analysis:

  • The candidate avoids answering the question about immigration reform 🚫
  • Diverts the topic completely to counter-terrorism 🛑

2. ⚖️ Fallacies of Weak Induction

🔍 What are Fallacies of Weak Induction?

Fallacies of weak induction occur when an argument is based on insufficient or weak evidence. These fallacies mostly appear in inductive reasoning, where the premises are related to the conclusion, but they don't provide strong enough support to justify it.

💡 The conclusion might be true, but the evidence is too weak, too biased, or drawn from unreliable sources.

🧩 Where They Occur:

These fallacies often appear in arguments based on:

  • Authority 👤
  • Predictions 🔮
  • Signs 🛑
  • Analogies 🔄
  • Generalizations 📊
  • Causal Inferences ⚙️

🚫 1. Appeal to Unqualified Authority (Argumentum ad Verecundiam)

📖 Latin Meaning: "Argument from respect / modesty"

This fallacy occurs when an argument relies on the opinion of someone who is not a legitimate expert on the topic in question.

📣 Just because someone is famous, powerful, or respected doesn't mean their opinion is valid in every field.

📌 Key Features:

  • The argument cites an authority figure 👤
  • That person lacks proper expertise on the topic discussed ❌
  • Unreliability can stem from: lack of expertise, bias/prejudice, motive to lie, lack of ability to perceive/recall, or personality problems ⚠️

📚 Examples:

📌 Example 1:

"It is always better to drink white wine with fish. Tony Blair says so, he must know what he is talking about, and he is the prime minister."

🎯 Argument Structure:

  • Tony Blair says white wine pairs with fish 🍷
  • Tony Blair is Prime Minister 🏛️
  • Assumption: Prime Ministers know everything about all subjects ❌
  • Conclusion: Therefore, white wine with fish is always better.

📌 Example 2:

"Tom Jones, a respected actor who plays the brilliant cardiologist Dr. John Smith in the film Emergency, recommends Drug X for improving the overall health of the heart.
👉 Therefore, it would be wise to take Drug X."

🎯 Analysis: An actor playing a doctor is not a real doctor and lacks legitimate medical authority ❌.

❓ 2. Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam)

📖 Latin Meaning: "Argument from ignorance"

This fallacy occurs when someone argues that a statement is true just because it hasn't been proven false, or false just because it hasn't been proven true.

🧠 Key Idea: An appeal to ignorance relies on the absence of evidence as if it were evidence of absence — but lack of proof is not proof in itself.

⚠️ Forms of This Fallacy:

  1. Claiming something is true because no one has proved it false ✔️➡️❓
  2. Claiming something is false because no one has proved it true ❌➡️❓

📚 Examples:

📌 Example 1:

"Nobody has ever proved to me there's a God, so I know there is no God."

📌 Example 2:

"After centuries of trying, no one has been able to prove that God does not exist.
👉 Therefore, God exists."

🎯 Analysis: The premises tell us nothing about existence or non-existence. The correct logical approach is to suspend judgment when proof is lacking.

✔️ Exceptions (When it is NOT a fallacy):

💡 Exception 1: Scientific/Expert Investigation

If qualified researchers thoroughly search for something within their expertise and find nothing, that fruitless search is positive evidence.

"Teams of scientists attempted over a number of decades to detect the existence of the luminiferous aether, and all failed to do so.
👉 Therefore, the luminiferous aether does not exist."

✔️ Strong Inductive Argument: Qualified search yielded no results.

💡 Exception 2: Simple Observation & Common Experience

"No one has ever seen Mr. Samuel drink a glass of wine, beer, or any other alcoholic beverage.
👉 Probably Mr. Samuel is a nondrinker."

✔️ Strong because drinking alcohol is observable and common.

💡 Exception 3: Courtroom Legal Presumption

"Members of the jury, you have heard the prosecution present its case against the defendant. Nothing, however, has been proved beyond a reasonable doubt.
👉 Therefore, under the law, the defendant is not guilty."

✔️ No Fallacy: In legal terms, "not guilty" specifically means "guilt beyond reasonable doubt was not proven."

⚠️ 3. Hasty Generalization (Converse Accident)

📖 Also known as Converse Accident

This fallacy happens when someone draws a broad conclusion based on a very limited, inadequate, or unrepresentative sample.

🧠 In short: Jumping to conclusions too quickly without enough data.

🔍 What Happens in This Fallacy?

  • Premise deals with a particular case/small sample 📊
  • Conclusion generalizes to a whole group or category 🌐
  • Proceeds from Particular → General (opposite of Accident, which goes General → Particular) 🔄

📚 Examples of Hasty Generalization:

📌 Example 1:

"I have met two persons in Hawassa town so far, and they were both nice to me.
👉 So, all people I will meet in Hawassa will be nice to me."

🎯 Analysis: Two people are not enough evidence to describe an entire town ❌.

📌 Example 2:

"Freshman Governance and Development Studies students of 2009 are 160 in number. Blood is taken out of three students and upon examination, all three have blood type 'B'.
👉 Therefore, the rest of the students will also have blood type 'B'."

🎯 Analysis: 3 out of 160 is an unrepresentative sample for blood types ❌.

✔️ Comparison Table: Accident vs. Converse Accident

Fallacy Type Direction of Reasoning Example Logic
Accident General → Particular Applying a general rule to a specific case where it doesn't fit.
Converse Accident
(Hasty Generalization)		 Particular → General Generalizing an entire group based on too few or unrepresentative cases.

✔️ When It's NOT a Fallacy (Valid Small Samples):

🧪 Valid Example 1:

"10 mg of Substance Z was fed to four mice and within 2 minutes all four went into shock and died.
👉 Probably Substance Z, in this amount, is fatal to the average mouse."

🎯 Why this is strong: Reaction is immediate and extreme; biological causal link holds across the species ✔️.

🍺 Valid Example 2:

"On three separate occasions I drank a bottle of Meta beer and found it flat and bitter.
👉 Probably I would find every bottle of Meta beer flat and bitter."

🎯 Why this is strong: Manufacturing quality control keeps beer taste constant from bottle to bottle ✔️.

🔥 4. The Fallacy of False Cause

This fallacy occurs when the causal link between premises and conclusion is unjustified, imagined, or oversimplified.

🧠 In short: Mistaking correlation for causation, or drawing a causal conclusion too quickly.

🚨 Common Problems with False Cause Arguments:

  • Confusing cause and effect 🔄
  • Oversimplifying complex causes ⚙️
  • Believing that preceding events automatically cause what comes next ⏱️
  • Confusing correlation with causation 📊

🧩 Three Types of False Cause Fallacies:

A. ⏱️ Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc Fallacy ("After this, therefore because of this")

📖 Latin Translation: "After this, therefore because of this"

Assumes that just because Event X happened before Event Y, Event X must have caused Event Y.

📊 Structure: Event X occurs before Event Y ➡️ Therefore, Event X caused Event Y.

Examples:

"During the last two months, the football team has worn red ribbons in their hair, and the team was defeated.
👉 Therefore, to prevent defeats in the future, the team should get rid of those red ribbons."
"Every time I wash the car, it starts to rain shortly afterwards.
👉 Therefore, my car-washing activities are causing outbursts of precipitation in the clouds."

🎯 Analysis: Clearly, wearing ribbons or washing a car does not cause rain or football losses 🌧️❌.

💡 Tip: Just because Y happened after X, it doesn't mean X caused Y.

B. ❌ Non Causa Pro Causa ("Not the cause for the cause")

📖 Latin Translation: "Not the cause for the cause"

Committed when something is wrongly identified as the cause of something else, but it has no actual causal connection (not based merely on temporal order).

Example 1:

"There are more churches & mosques in Ethiopia today than ever before, and more HIV victims than ever before.
👉 So, to eliminate the pandemic we must abolish churches & mosques."

🎯 Analysis: This is a correlation, not causation. Both increases are due to population growth, not churches causing disease ❌.

Example 2:

"Successful business executives are paid salaries in excess of $5,000.
👉 Therefore, the best way to ensure that Ferguson will become a successful executive is to raise his salary to at least $5,000."

🎯 Analysis: Mistakes the effect for the cause! Executive success causes high salary, not vice versa ❌.

C. 🧩 Oversimplified Cause

Occurs when a multitude of causes is responsible for an outcome, but the arguer selects just one single cause and presents it as the sole explanation.

⚖️ Fallacies of Weak Induction – Short Summary

🔍 Definition: Arguments where the premises are related to the conclusion but provide insufficient, biased, or unreliable support. The conclusion might be true, but the reasoning is flawed.

📌 6 Key Types:

  1. 👤 Appeal to Unqualified Authority: Using a non-expert's opinion as evidence.
    Example: "A celebrity says this diet works, so it must be true!"
  2. ❓ Appeal to Ignorance: Claiming something is true because it hasn't been disproven (or vice versa).
    Example: "No one's proved aliens don't exist, so they must!"
  3. 📊 Hasty Generalization: Drawing a broad conclusion from a small/unrepresentative sample.
    Example: "I met two rude Parisians, so all French people are rude!"
  4. ⏳ False Cause (3 Subtypes):
    • Post Hoc: Assuming "X caused Y" just because X came first.
    • Non Causa Pro Causa: Wrongly blaming one thing for another.
    • Oversimplified Cause: Ignoring multiple factors and picking just one.
  5. 🛷 Slippery Slope: Claiming one small event will lead to a chain reaction of disaster without proof.
  6. 🔄 Weak Analogy: Comparing two things that are not similar in relevant respects.

🔥 Over Simplified Cause Fallacy

This variety of false cause fallacy is more probably committed than the other two varieties. The Over simplified cause fallacy occurs when a large number of causes are responsible for an effect, but the arguer selects just one of these causes and represents it as if it is the sole cause of the event.

📚 Example:

"The quality of education in our grade schools and high schools has been declining for years. Clearly our teachers just are not doing their jobs these days."

🎯 Analysis:

The argument of the above example commits over simplified cause fallacy. For the reason that in this argument the cause for the declining of the quality of education is not limited to one single cause though there are many factors that can be considered as the cause for this effect. For instance, to mention some of factors that are responsible for the decline quality of education are: lack of discipline in the home; parental un-involvement; and, drug use by students, and etc.

🛷 5. The Fallacy of Slippery Slope

The fallacy of slippery slope occurs when we assume that a series of events happen, after one other event as a result of the first cause. This fallacy is occurred when a certain argument rests on chains of events and the arguer fails to provide sufficient reasons why this chain of events committed. In other words, it is committed when one affirms an unjustifiable “chain reaction” of causes which, if it is allowed to continue leads inevitably to disaster.

🔗 Key Characteristics:

  • Based on a chain reaction of events ⚙️
  • The connection between steps is not proven ❌
  • Leads to an unjustified or exaggerated conclusion 📉

📚 Example:

"I know the impetus for the whole tragedy in her life. She was jobless and has no other choice but to join bar ladies. While she was working in bars, she becomes infected with HIV/AIDS. Then, she becomes bedridden patient and in the lost her life. All these misfortune fall up on her due to her dismissal from the university in the first semesters of the first year."

🎯 Analysis:

The arguer, in the example, associated the death of a girl with her failure in the university examination, without considering other factors that lead her to join bar ladies, such as poverty, the problem of parents that could advise her to lead a good life even after she failed to pass national examination, and so on.

🔄 6. The Fallacy of Weak Analogy

The fallacy of weak analogy is an inductive argument in which the conclusion depends on the existence of analogy, or similarities between two things. Argument based on analogy would be strong when either property cited, as relevant between two or more things, or when relevant differences between the objects are taken into consideration. When these requirements are failed, the inductive argument becomes weak.

The fallacy of weak analogy is committed when important differences between two things or more things compared are not real similar in the relevant respects or when the analogy is not strong enough to support the conclusion.

📐 Logical Form:

Object “A” has attributes a, b, c, and z.
Object “B” has attributes a, b, c.
------------------------------------
Therefore, object B probably has attributes z also.

📚 Example 1:

"Kebede’s new car is bright blue in color and has leather upholstery and gets excellent gas mileage. Taye’s new car is also bright blue in color and has leather upholstery. Therefore, it probably gets excellent gas mileage, too."

🎯 Analysis: The color of a car and the choice of upholstery have nothing to do with gasoline consumption. Color and leather seats are unrelated to fuel efficiency.

📚 Example 2:

"I do not see what all the argument is about guns. Of course gun ownership should not be prohibited. You can kill someone with a cricket bat, but no one proposes to ban ownership of cricket bats."

🎯 Structure & Analysis:

  • Guns are like cricket bats in that both can be used to kill people.
  • Whenever an object “X” is similar to an object “Y” in one respect, it is similar in all respects ❌
  • Objects that are similar to each other in all respects should be treated identically
  • We would not ban ownership of cricket bats ✔️
  • Therefore, we should not ban ownership of guns.

🧩 3.4 Fallacies of Presumption: Definition and Types

The fallacies of presumption include four different types of fallacies, namely: begging the question, complex question, false dichotomy, and suppressed evidence. The fallacies of presumption arise not because the premises are irrelevant to the conclusion or provided insufficient reason for believing the conclusion. These fallacies committed when the arguer provides an argument that has premises which try to presume what they purport to prove.

The fallacies of presumption frequently have tricky and confusing phraseologies for the purpose of concealing or hiding the wrong ideas stated in the premise, even though the ideas stated in the premises are not supported by logical evidence or proof, the arguer invites readers or listeners to accept his or her argument as if it does not need proof or evidence.

💡 Short Summary – Fallacies of Presumption:

Errors where an argument assumes what it's trying to prove or hides critical flaws in its reasoning. Unlike weak induction, the problem isn't lack of evidence—it's unjustified assumptions or manipulative framing.

📌 4 Key Types:

  1. 🔄 1. Begging the Question (Circular Reasoning): The conclusion is assumed in the premise.
    Example: "The Bible is true because it says so."
  2. ❓ 2. Complex Question (Loaded Question): A question traps the answerer by hiding an assumption.
    Example: "Have you stopped lying?" (Assumes you lied before.)
  3. ⚫⚪ 3. False Dichotomy (Either-Or Fallacy): Presents only two options when more exist.
    Example: "Either support war or hate freedom."
  4. 📉 4. Suppressed Evidence: Ignores facts that would weaken the argument.
    Example: "This medicine works—look at this one person!" (Ignores 99 failed trials.)

🔄 1. Begging the Question Fallacy (Petitio Principii)

(Latin: Petitio Principii – “Assuming the initial point”)

The fallacy of begging the question occurs when an arguer uses some form of phraseology that tends to conceal the questionably true character of a key premise. To make it clear, this fallacy is committed when the arguer, without providing real evidence, asks the readers or listeners to simply accept the conclusion of his or her argument. Some times this argument is known as circular reasoning since the argument depends upon premises that state the same thing as the conclusion.

🔍 Definition Recap:

  • The conclusion is assumed in the premise
  • The argument offers no real evidence
  • The premise and conclusion say the same thing but in different words
Premise: X is true
Conclusion: Therefore, X is true

📚 Example 1:

"I believe the prime minister is telling the truth since he says he is telling the truth."

🎯 Analysis: It ignores an important premise which is needed to make the argument acceptable. Proof is not given on the truth of the prime minister’s speech. Even though the arguer does not give proof, he or she begs us to accept it as true as if it does not need proof.

📚 Example 2:

"Capital punishment is justified for crimes of murder and kidnapping because it is quite legitimate and appropriate that someone be put to death for having committed such hateful and inhuman acts."

🎯 Analysis: The arguer has really said the same thing twice. To say that capital punishment is “justified” means the same thing as to say that it is “legitimate” and “appropriate”. The premise and conclusion mean the same thing, failing to give real reasons why capital punishment is justified.

❓ 2. The Fallacy of Complex or Loaded Question

This happens when the conclusion (that is, answer) is supported by confusing and tricky questions (that is, premises). This fallacy is committed when a single question that is really two or more questions is asked and a single answer is then applied to both questions.

🧠 Why it's tricky: No matter how the respondent answers, it appears as if they are admitting something they may not have intended to.

🔍 Definition Recap:

  • Presumes something unproven or controversial
  • Combines two questions into one
  • Traps the respondent into seeming guilty or wrong either way

❓ Example:

"Have you stopped cheating on exams?"

🎯 Analysis of Answers:

  • If the respondent answers "Yes" → Implies you cheated in the past and have stopped now.
  • If the respondent answers "No" → Implies you cheated in the past and are continuing to cheat now.

🎯 What's wrong? The question presumes that the person has cheated without any proof. In reality, it contains two hidden questions:

  1. Did you cheat on exams in the past?
  2. If you did cheat in the past, have you stopped now?

⚫⚪ 3. The Fallacy of False Dichotomy

The fallacy of false dichotomy can be also known as “false bifurcation”, false dilemma, black and white thinking, and “either…or…fallacy”.

This fallacy is committed when the premise of an argument is an either… or… statement or a disjunctive statement that presents two alternatives as if they were jointly exhaustive (as if no third alternative was possible).

To make more precise, it occurs when a person provides two alternatives, which are false, as the only option in the argument and then eliminates one alternative so it seems we are left with only one option—the one the arguer wanted us to choose. But, there are many different alternatives that the arguer fails to provide.

📚 Example:

"Well, it is time for a decision. Will you contribute $10 to our environmental fund, or are you on the side of environmental destruction?"

🎯 Analysis:

The argument allows us only two options: contribute $10 to the fund or be in favor of environmental destruction. This argument commits false dichotomy because the two options are not exhaustive. There are other possibilities such as contributing less than $10, or contributing nothing but supporting environmental protection by other means. The arguer misleads the listener into thinking the disjunctive premise is true by necessity.

📉 4. The Fallacy of Suppressed Evidence

❓ What is Suppressed Evidence?

The fallacy of suppressed evidence is committed when the inductive argument deliberately (or unintentionally) ignores or omits some important piece of evidence that fails to support his or her position and emphasizes some other reasons that are not as important to the conclusion of the argument.

🔍 Definition Recap:

  • Important facts are left out or ignored
  • The omitted evidence could change the conclusion
  • The argument becomes biased or misleading because of these omissions

📝 Example:

"Hawassa University is the best university in Ethiopia; because it has very fat and tall teachers, finest buildings and a number of students."

🎯 Analysis:

✔️ The argument mentions superficial factors (fat/tall teachers, buildings, student quantity).

❌ Key evidence omitted includes the organization of the university, the qualification and experience of instructors, equipment available for instruction, and student services. De-emphasizing these important cases while focusing on insignificant evidence commits the fallacy of suppressed evidence.

🗣️ Linguistic Fallacies

Linguistic fallacies are the result of a misuse of language, such as incorrect use of words, grammatical lack of clarity, vagueness and other linguistic impressions.

There are two main types of linguistic fallacies:

  • 3.5.1 Fallacies of Ambiguity 🌀
  • 3.5.2 Fallacies of Grammatical Analogy 🧱

🌀 3.5.1 Fallacies of Ambiguity

Fallacies of ambiguity arise from the occurrence of some form of ambiguity in either the premises or the conclusion (or both). They are committed when misleading or wrong conclusions of an argument are drawn from ambiguous words or sentences. The fallacies of ambiguity include two types: equivocation and amphiboly.

🔄 Fallacies of Ambiguity - Short Summary:

Errors caused by unclear or shifting language, leading to misleading conclusions.

📌 Two Main Types:

  1. 🗣️ Equivocation: Using a word with multiple meanings as if it meant the same thing throughout.
    Example: "Feathers are light. Light things can't be dark. ∴ Feathers can't be dark." (Here, "light" shifts between "not heavy" and "bright.")
  2. 🌪️ Amphiboly: Exploiting grammatical ambiguity (poor sentence structure) to mislead.
    Example: "The police shot the thief with a gun." (Who had the gun? Ambiguous phrasing implies the thief did, but the police likely did.)

A. Equivocation Fallacy 🗣️

The fallacy of equivocation occurs when the conclusion of an argument depends on the fact that one or more words are used in two different senses in the argument.

📚 Example 1:

"Odd things arouse human suspicion. But seventeen is an odd number. Therefore, seventeen arouses human suspicion."

🎯 Analysis: The word "odd" shifts meaning. In premise 1, it means 'strange'. In premise 2, it means 'a number not divisible by two'.

📚 Example 2:

"Any law can be repealed by the legislative authority. But the law of gravity is a law. Therefore, the law of gravity can be repealed by the legislative authority."

🎯 Analysis: Equivocates on the word "law". Premise 1 refers to 'statutory law', while premise 2 refers to a 'law of nature'.

📚 Example 3:

"All stars are in orbit in outer space. Sarah Flamingo is a star. Therefore, Sarah Flamingo is in orbit in outer space."

🎯 Analysis: Shift of meaning of 'star'. Premise 1 means 'luminous celestial body'. Premise 2 means 'entertainment celebrity'.

💡 Relative Terms: Equivocation can also involve relative terms like “small”, “good”, “bad”, “light”, “heavy”, “difficult”, “easy”, “tall”, “short” which shift meaning in different contexts.

📚 Example 4:

"A mouse is an animal. Therefore, a large mouse is a large animal."

🎯 Analysis: The word "large" is a relative term and means different things depending on context.

B. Amphiboly Fallacy 🌪️

The fallacy of amphiboly is caused by an error in grammatical construction of statements that can be interpreted in two or more distinctly different ways without making clear which meaning is intended. It is a structural defect due to a mistake in grammar or punctuation—a missing comma, a dangling modifier, an ambiguous antecedent of a pronoun, or careless arrangement of words.

📚 Example 1:

"Solomon told Dawit that he had made a mistake. It follows that Solomon has at least the courage to admit his own mistakes."

🎯 Analysis: The pronoun "he" has an ambiguous antecedent; it can refer either to Solomon or Dawit.

📚 Example 2:

"Our engineering school teachers told us how to build a house in three years."

🎯 Analysis: Can be interpreted in two ways: "teaching us for three years how to build a house" OR "building a house within a three-year construction timeframe".

⚠️ Legal & Practical Risks (Contracts and Wills):

Two areas where cases of amphiboly cause serious problems involve contracts and wills.

📜 Will Example:

"Mrs. Sosna stated in her will, 'I leave my 500-carat diamond necklace and my pet cat to Hana and Bethlehem.' Therefore, we conclude that Hana gets the necklace and Bethlehem gets the cat."

🎯 Analysis: The conclusion favors Hana. Bethlehem could argue the gifts should be shared equally. Adding "respectively" or "collectively" avoids dispute.

📜 Contract Example:

"Mr. Markos signed a contract that reads, 'In exchange for painting my house, I promise to pay Asenafi $5000 and give him my new Cadillac only if he finishes the job by May 1.' Therefore, since Asenafi did not finish until May 10, it follows that he gets neither the $5000 nor the Cadillac."

🎯 Analysis: The conclusion favors Mr. Markos. Asenafi will argue May 1 affected only the Cadillac, entitled to $5000. Properly inserting a comma fixes the ambiguity.

🧱 5. Fallacy of Grammatical Analogy

The fallacies of grammatical analogy arise when reasoning incorrectly transfers characteristics between parts and wholes (or vice versa), based on grammatical or linguistic structure.

📌 These arguments look like they follow sound logic because they resemble valid arguments in form, but they're flawed in reasoning.

📖 Definition:

Fallacies of grammatical analogy occur when:

  • 🧱 The parts of something are wrongly assumed to describe the whole, or
  • 🏗️ The whole is wrongly assumed to describe each of its parts.

These fallacies are structurally similar to good arguments, but the conclusion is based on a false transfer of attributes.

🧠 Two Main Types:

  1. Fallacy of Composition (Parts → Whole)
  2. Fallacy of Division (Whole → Parts)

🔹 A. Fallacy of Composition 🧩

The fallacy of composition is committed when the arguer wrongly transfers the attributes of the parts of something onto the whole. In other words, it is committed when someone argues that what is true of each part of a whole is also (necessarily) true of the whole itself, or what is true of some parts is true of the whole.

❌ Illegitimate Transference Examples:

1. "Every sentence in this paragraph is well written. Therefore, the paragraph is well written."

2. "Each atom in a piece of chalk is invisible. Therefore, the chalk is invisible."

🎯 Analysis: Transference of "well written" and "invisible" from parts to whole is illegitimate and fallacious.

✔️ Legitimate Transference Examples (NOT Fallacies):

1. "Every atom in this piece of chalk has mass. Therefore, the piece of chalk has mass."

2. "Every component in this picket fence is white. Therefore, the whole fence is white."

🎯 Analysis: Here, attributes like having mass or being white legitimately carry over from parts to the whole.

🔹 B. Fallacy of Division ✂️

The fallacy of division is the direct opposite or converse of composition. The fallacy of division is committed when attributes are wrongly transferred from whole to parts. It is committed when someone argues that what is true of a whole is also (necessarily) true of its parts, or what is true of a whole is true of some of its parts.

❌ Illegitimate Transference Examples:

1. "This chalk is visible. Therefore, each atom in a piece of chalk is visible."

2. "The USA is the wealthiest country in the world. Hence, my uncle who lives there must be wealthy."

🎯 Analysis: The arguer considers collective properties of the class as distributive properties of individual parts. A whole represents something different from its parts.

✔️ Legitimate Transference Examples (NOT Fallacies):

1. "This piece of chalk has mass. Therefore, the atoms that compose this piece of chalk have mass."

2. "This field of poppies is uniformly orange in color. Therefore, the individual poppies are orange in color."

🎯 Analysis: Legitimate transfer of physical characteristics from whole to parts containing no fallacy.
