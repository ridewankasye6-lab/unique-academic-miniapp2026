📘 CHAPTER FIVE: MOTIVATION AND EMOTIONS

Motivation Definitions, Theories, Conflict of Motives, Frustration & Theories of Emotions

🔥 5.1 Motivation

🧠 5.1.1 Definition and Types of Motivation

✅ What is Motivation?
Motivation is the internal force or factor that initiates, directs, and persists peoples' behavior towards a purpose.
  • 🚀 Initiates behavior: Starts it.
  • 🎯 Directs behavior: Guides it toward a goal.
  • ⏳ Sustains behavior: Keeps it going until the goal is reached.

In simple terms, motivation is what drives us to take action and keeps us going until we achieve something.

📌 Example: Hunger 🍽️
For example, hunger causes a person to get up, go into the kitchen, and search for something to eat.
  • You feel hungry → this starts your behavior (getting up).
  • You go to the kitchen → this directs your action toward the goal (finding food).
  • You keep searching or cooking until you eat → this sustains the behavior.
So, hunger is the motivational force that initiates the action, directs it, and sustains it.

⚙️ Types of Motivation

It is possible to categorize motivation into two main types:

  • Intrinsic Motivation: A type of motivation in which a person acts because the act itself is rewarding or satisfying in some internal manner (for enjoyment or internal satisfaction).
  • Extrinsic Motivation: A type of motivation in which individuals act because the action leads to an outcome that is external to a person (such as money, grades, or concrete rewards).

Motives are also frequently divided into:

  • (a) Physiological, primary, or organic motives: Needs essential for survival such as hunger, thirst, and need for sleep.
  • (b) Personal, social, or secondary motives: Learned motives such as affiliation, competition, and individual interests & goals.

🧩 5.1.2 Approaches to Motivation (Theories of Motivation)

Theoretical approaches explain the sources of our motivation. Key theories include instinct, drive-reduction, arousal, incentive, cognitive, and humanistic approaches.

a) Instinct Approaches to Motivation

  • Focused on biologically determined and innate patterns of both human and animal behavior.
  • It realizes that some human behavior is controlled by hereditary factors.
  • Example: Just as animals are governed by instincts to reproduce, migrate, build nests, mate, and protect territory, early researchers proposed humans are governed similarly.
  • Early theorists listed thousands of human instincts including curiosity, flight (running away), pugnacity (aggressiveness), and acquisition (gathering possessions).

b) Drive-Reduction Approaches to Motivation

  • Need: A requirement of some material (such as food or water) essential for the survival of the organism.
  • Drive: A psychological tension and physical arousal arising from a need that motivates an organism to fulfill the need and reduce tension.
  • Proposes a connection between internal psychological states and outward behavior.
  • Two kinds of drives:
    • Primary drives: Involve survival needs of the body, such as hunger and thirst.
    • Secondary (acquired) drives: Learned through experience or conditioning, such as the need for money or social approval.
  • Includes the concept of Homeostasis: The tendency of the body to maintain a steady state or equilibrium.

c) Arousal Approaches: Beyond Drive Reduction

  • Seek to explain behavior where the goal is to maintain or increase excitement and stimulation.
  • Suggests each person tries to maintain a certain optimal level of stimulation and activity.
  • If stimulation levels become too high, we try to reduce them; if levels become too low, we seek stimulation to increase them.

d) Incentive Approaches: Motivation’s Pull

  • Suggests motivation stems from the desire to attain external rewards, known as incentives.
  • External stimuli (such as grades, money, affection, food, or sex) account for motivation.
  • Push vs. Pull: Internal drives push behavior while external incentives pull behavior. Drives and incentives work together in motivating behavior rather than contradicting each other.

e) Cognitive Approaches: The Thoughts Behind Motivation

  • Suggests motivation is a result of people’s thoughts, beliefs, expectations, and goals.
  • Example: Motivation to study for a test is based on the expectation of how well studying will pay off in terms of a good grade.
  • Differentiates between:
    • Intrinsic motivation: Participating in an activity for personal enjoyment.
    • Extrinsic motivation: Doing something for concrete external rewards like money or grades.

f) Humanistic Approaches to Motivation (Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs)

Abraham Maslow rejected psychoanalysis and behaviorism in favor of a humanistic view, proposing that behavior is influenced by a hierarchy of five classes of needs. Lower-level needs must be satisfied before higher-level needs motivate behavior.

📜 Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (Bottom to Top):
  1. 1. Physiological Needs 🍔: Basic biological requirements for human survival (e.g., air, food, drink, water, shelter, warmth, sex, sleep). If unmet, motivation is focused entirely on satisfying them.
  2. 2. Safety Needs 🛡️: Protection from elements, security, order, law, financial safety, stability, health stability, freedom from fear. Example: Seeking a stable job, health insurance, or living in a safe neighborhood.
  3. 3. Love and Belongingness Needs ❤️: Social needs involving feelings of belongingness, friendship, intimacy, family, community, trust, acceptance, and affection.
  4. 4. Esteem Needs 🏆: Need to be respected as a useful, honorable individual. Divided into:
    • (i) Esteem for oneself (dignity, achievement, mastery, independence).
    • (ii) Desire for reputation or respect from others (status, prestige).
  5. 5. Self-Actualization Needs 🌟: Realizing personal potential, self-fulfillment, seeking personal growth, and peak experiences. The desire "to become everything one is capable of becoming".

⚔️ 5.1.3 Conflict of Motives and Frustration

When choosing among motives, especially important decisions, internal conflict and indecision arise from motivational pushes and pulls.

📌 Four Basic Types of Motivational Conflicts:

  • 1. Approach-Approach Conflict 😇 vs 😇:
    • 📌 Definition: Choosing between two desirable options. You can only pick one, even though both are good.
    • 🎬 Example: Wanting to go to a movie 🎥 or a concert 🎵 at the same time. Both are attractive, but you must choose one.
    • 💡 Often easiest to resolve, but still causes some hesitation.
  • 2. Avoidance-Avoidance Conflict 😰 vs 😰:
    • 📌 Definition: Choosing between two undesirable options. No matter what you choose, it's unpleasant.
    • 🏚️ Example: Choosing between selling the family home 🏠 or declaring bankruptcy 💸. Both are negative, but one must be chosen.
    • 💡 Very stressful and often leads to delays or procrastination.
  • 3. Approach-Avoidance Conflict 😍 vs 😖:
    • 📌 Definition: A single goal or event has both attractive (positive) and unattractive (negative) features. You are attracted to it but also hesitant.
    • 🎓 Example: A freshman student wants to start dating, but at the same time worries it will consume her study time.
  • 4. Multiple Approach-Avoidance Conflict:
    • 📌 Definition: Choosing between two or more alternatives where each alternative has both positive and negative features.
    • 💼 Example: Choosing between two jobs. Job A pays a high salary at a well-known company but requires long hours and relocation to a miserable climate. Job B offers advancement and benefits in a better climate, but pays less and has an unpredictable schedule.

🎭 5.2 Emotions

5.2.1 Definition of Emotion

  • Emotion: A negative or positive feeling generally in reaction to stimuli that are accompanied by physiological arousal and related behavior.
  • It is a complex reaction pattern involving experiential, behavioral, and physiological elements to deal with a personally significant matter or event.
  • It represents the feeling aspect of our consciousness.

🧩 Components of Emotion

  • 1. Physical Component (Physiological Arousal): Internal body state accompanying emotion created by the sympathetic nervous system. Heart rate increases, breathing accelerates, pupils dilate, and mouth becomes dry.
  • 2. Behavioral Component: Outward expressions such as facial expressions, gestures, body posture, tone of voice, fighting, running, kissing, or yelling. While facial expressions vary across cultures, some aspects are universal.
  • 3. Cognitive Component (Subjective Experience / Labeling): Interpreting the subjective feeling by giving it a label (e.g., anger, fear, happiness) based on memories of previous experiences and context. Labels are learned responses influenced by language and culture.

✅ The 6 Basic Emotions

Biologically rooted, universal, and easily recognized in facial expressions across all cultures and age groups:

  1. 😃 Happiness
  2. 😲 Surprise
  3. 😢 Sadness
  4. 😨 Fear
  5. 🤢 Disgust
  6. 😡 Anger

📖 Theories of Emotion

"What comes first: the body's reaction or the feeling itself?"
  • I. James-Lange Theory of Emotion:
    • Proposes that a stimulus produces a physiological reaction (arousal), which then leads to the labeling of the emotion.
    • Sequence: Stimulus → Physical Arousal → Emotion.
    • 💬 "I am afraid because I am aroused / running."
  • II. Cannon-Bard Theory of Emotion:
    • Proposes that emotional feeling and physiological arousal occur at the same time, not one after the other.
    • Sequence: Stimulus → Physical Arousal + Emotion (Simultaneous).
    • 💬 "I am afraid and running and aroused at the same time!"
  • III. Schachter-Singer Cognitive Arousal Theory:
    • Proposes two things must happen simultaneously before emotion is felt: physical arousal and cognitive labeling/interpretation of that arousal.
    • Sequence: Stimulus → Physical Arousal + Cognitive Interpretation → Emotion.
    • 💬 "I am aroused in the presence of a scary dog; therefore, I must be afraid."
Theory Process Sequence Core Concept
James-Lange Stimulus → Arousal → Emotion Body reaction causes emotional feeling.
Cannon-Bard Stimulus → Arousal + Emotion Body reaction and emotion occur simultaneously.
Schachter-Singer Stimulus → Arousal + Cognition → Emotion Arousal + cognitive appraisal determine emotion.
