🔍 4.1. Basic Concepts of Critical Thinking

4.1.1. Meaning of Critical Thinking

Critical thinking can be defined as:

  • ✔️ A wide range of cognitive skills and intellectual dispositions needed to effectively identify, analyze, and evaluate arguments and truth claims.
  • ✔️ Involving or exercising skilled judgment or observation i.e. Thinking clearly and intelligently.
  • ✔️ A process or journey that helps us:
    • ✅ Arrive at the most useful, helpful, and most likely destinations when evaluating claims for scientific truth;
    • ✅ Formulate and present convincing reasons in support of conclusions; and
    • ✅ Make reasonable, intelligent decisions about what to believe and what to do.
👉 Therefore, critical thinking means:
  • • Thinking clearly
  • • Thinking fairly
  • • Thinking rationally
  • • Thinking objectively
  • • Thinking independently

🎯 Therefore, the aim of critical thinking is to arrive at well-reasoned, considered, and justifiable conclusions.

👨‍🏫 Philosophical Definitions of Critical Thinking

1. John Dewey (American Philosopher)

Defined critical thinking as an active, persistent, and careful consideration of a belief or supposed form of knowledge.

  • 'Active': Refers to thinking things through for yourself, raising questions yourself, finding relevant information yourself, rather than learning in a largely passive way from someone else.
  • 'Persistent' & 'Careful consideration': Dewey contrasts critical thinking with the unreflective thinking we all sometimes engage in (such as jumping to conclusions or making quick decisions without thinking). What matters are the reasons we have for believing something and the implications of our beliefs.

2. Edward Glaser

Defined critical thinking as:

  1. An attitude of being disposed to consider in a thoughtful way the problems and subjects that come within the range of one’s experience;
  2. Knowledge of the methods of logical enquiry and reasoning; and
  3. Some skill in applying those methods.

3. Robert Ennis

Defined critical thinking as reasonable, reflective thinking that is focused on deciding what to believe or do. Decision-making ("deciding what to do") is an important part of critical thinking in Ennis's conception.

4. Richard Paul

Defined critical thinking as a mode of thinking about any subject, content, or problem in which the thinker improves the quality of his or her thinking by skillful thinking and imposing intellectual standards upon them. Paul associates critical thinking with reflecting on thoughts.

5. Michael Scriven

Defined critical thinking as skilled and active interpretation and evaluation of observations and communications, information and argumentation.

  • Interpretation: Of texts, speech, film, graphics, actions, and body language helps construct and select the best alternatives.
  • Evaluation: Evaluating the truth, probability, or reliability of claims. Evaluation is the process of determining the merit, quality, worth, or value of something. According to Scriven, to be critical, thinking has to meet certain standards like clarity, relevance, reasonableness, and so on.
💡 Criticocreative Thinking: Critical thinking is sometimes referred to as "criticocreative" thinking—a combination of critical and creative thinking. It is an evaluative thinking concerned with the quality of reasoning or argument presented in support of a belief or course of action.

✅ Standards of Critical Thinking

👉 Why do we need standards?
To distinguish critical thinking from uncritical thinking, we use certain intellectual standards. These standards help ensure our thinking is clear, rational, and fair.

📌 The most important intellectual standards are:

1️⃣ Clarity 🔍
Refers to clear understanding of concepts and clearly expressing them in a language that is free of obscurity and vagueness.

2️⃣ Precision 🎯
Refers to a matter of being exact, accurate, and careful. Most ideas are vague and obscure though we think we have precise understanding of them.

3️⃣ Accuracy 📍
Refers to correct/genuine information. Decisions based on wrong and false information are likely to result in distorting realities.

4️⃣ Relevance 🔗
Refers to the connections of ideas. Critical thinkers carefully choose only the information that has logical relation with the ideas at hand.

5️⃣ Consistency ⚖️
Refers to the quality of having the same opinions or standards. Logic tells us that if a person holds inconsistent beliefs, at least one of those beliefs must be false. There are two kinds of inconsistency to avoid:

  • • Logical Inconsistency: Involves saying or believing inconsistent things (things that cannot both or all be true) about a particular matter.
  • • Practical Inconsistency: Involves saying one thing and doing another.

6️⃣ Logical Correctness 🧠
When the combinations of thoughts are mutually supporting and make sense in combination, the thinking is logical. To think logically is to reason correctly, using accurate and well-supported beliefs.

7️⃣ Completeness 📖
Deep and complete thinking are more preferable than shallow and superficial thinking. Thinking is better when it is deep rather than shallow, thorough rather than superficial.

8️⃣ Fairness ⚖️
Refers to being open-minded, impartial, and free of distorting biases and preconceptions.

📜 Principles of Good Argument

To evaluate arguments effectively, critical thinking relies on fundamental structural and logical principles:

1. The Structural Principle 🏗️
An argument should meet the fundamental structural requirements of a well-formed argument. The conclusion either follows necessarily from its premises (deductive) or follows probably from its premises (inductive).

2. The Relevance Principle 🔗
An argument should set forth only reasons whose truth provides some evidence for the truth of the conclusion. Premises must be relevant to the conclusion.

3. The Acceptability Principle ✅
The reasons set forth in support of a conclusion must be acceptable to a reasonable audience.

4. The Sufficiency Principle 📊
An argument should attempt to provide relevant and acceptable reasons of the right kind, that together are sufficient in number and weight to justify the acceptance of the conclusion.

5. The Rebuttal Principle 🛡️
An argument should include an effective rebuttal to all anticipated serious criticisms that may be brought against it.

👉 Principles of Critical Thinking

👉 Critical thinking requires not just skills, but also guiding principles that ensure fairness, clarity, and truth-seeking during discussions and arguments.

1️⃣ The Fallibility Principle 👤
Each participant in a discussion of a disputed issue should be willing to accept the fact that he or she is imperfect. One must acknowledge that one’s own initial view may not be the most defensible position on the question.

2️⃣ The Truth Seeking Principle 🔍
Each participant should be committed to the task of seriously searching for the truth. One should be willing to examine alternative positions seriously and look for insights in the positions of others.

3️⃣ The Clarity Principle 💡
Requires that the formulations of all positions, defenses, and attacks should be free of any kind of linguistic confusion and clearly separated from other positions and issues.

4️⃣ The Burden of Proof Principle ⚖️
Requires that the burden of proof for any position usually rests on the participant who sets forth the position. If an opponent asks, the proponent should provide an argument for that position.

5️⃣ The Principle of Charity 🤲
If a participant’s argument is reformulated by an opponent, it should be carefully expressed in its strongest possible version that is consistent with what is believed to be the original intention of the arguer. The arguer should be given the benefit of the doubt.

6️⃣ The Suspension of Judgment Principle ⏸️
If no position is defended by a good argument, or if two or more positions seem to be defended with equal strength, one should suspend judgment about the issue. If evidence is lacking, wait until there is more basis for a decision.

7️⃣ The Resolution Principle 🏁
An issue should be considered resolved if the argument is structurally sound, uses relevant and acceptable reasons, provides sufficient grounds, and includes an effective rebuttal to all serious criticisms.

🌟 Characteristics of Critical Thinking

🌟 Basic Traits of Critical Thinkers

Critical thinking is not only about skills but also about dispositions, attitudes, habits, and values that critical thinkers consistently show.

💡 Critical thinkers:

  • ✔️ Are honest with themselves: They openly acknowledge what they don't know, recognize their own limitations, and watch for their own mistakes.
  • ✔️ Regard problems and controversial issues as exciting challenges: Instead of avoiding difficult topics, they see them as opportunities to learn and grow.
  • ✔️ Strive for understanding: They keep curiosity alive, remain patient with complexity, and invest the time needed to overcome confusion.
  • ✔️ Base judgments on evidence, not personal preference: They defer judgment whenever evidence is insufficient and revise judgments when new evidence reveals error.
  • ✔️ Are interested in other people's ideas: Even when they tend to disagree with the other person.
  • ✔️ Avoid extreme views: They recognize that extreme views (whether conservative or liberal) are seldom correct, practice fair-mindedness, and seek a balanced view.
  • ✔️ Practice restraint: Controlling their feelings rather than being controlled by them, and thinking before acting.

❌ Basic Traits of Uncritical Thinkers

  • ❌ Believe they know more than they do, ignore their limitations, and assume their views are error-free.
  • ❌ Regard problems and controversial issues as nuisances or threats to their ego.
  • ❌ Are impatient with complexity and prefer remaining confused over making the effort to understand.
  • ❌ Base judgments on first impressions and gut/instinctive reactions; tend to follow feelings.
  • ❌ Are preoccupied with themselves and their own opinions, unwilling to pay attention to others' views. At the first sign of disagreement, they think, "How can I refute this?"
  • ❌ Ignore the need for balance and give preference to views that support their established views.

📊 Key Intellectual Traits Comparison

Critical Thinkers 🧠 Uncritical Thinkers ❌
Passionate drive for clarity, precision, accuracy, and standards. Unclear, imprecise, inaccurate, and careless.
Sensitive to impediments like egocentrism and sociocentrism. Fall prey to egocentrism, sociocentrism, and relativistic thinking.
Skilled at understanding, analyzing, and evaluating arguments. Misunderstand or evaluate arguments unfairly; draw unsupported conclusions.
Intellectually honest; acknowledge limitations. Pretend they know more than they do; ignore limitations.
Listen open-mindedly to opposing points of view. Closed-minded and resist criticisms.
Base beliefs on facts and evidence; aware of personal biases. Base beliefs on personal preferences, self-interest, and lack bias awareness.
Think independently; avoid blindly following group opinion. Engage in "groupthink" and uncritically follow the crowd.
Have intellectual courage to face challenging ideas. Fear and resist ideas that challenge basic beliefs.
Pursue truth despite obstacles and difficulties. Indifferent to truth and lack curiosity.

🚧 Barriers to Critical Thinking

Several mental habits and biases act as major barriers to rational thought:

1️⃣ Egocentrism 🪞

The tendency to see reality as centered on oneself. Egocentrics are self-absorbed people who view their interests, ideas, and values as superior to everyone else’s.

  • • Self-Interested Thinking: The tendency to accept and defend beliefs that harmonize with one’s self-interest.
    💡 Example: Most doctors support legislation making it more difficult for them to be sued for malpractice because they do not want to be punished for workplace mistakes.
  • • Superiority Bias (Better-Than-Average Effect): The tendency to overrate oneself—seeing oneself as better in some respect than one actually is.

2️⃣ Sociocentrism 👥

Group-centered thinking that hinders rational thinking by focusing excessively on the group.

  • • Group Bias: The tendency to see one’s own group (nation, tribe, sect, peer group) as inherently better than others. Usually absorbed unconsciously from early childhood.
  • • Conformism: The tendency to follow the crowd unthinkingly out of adherence to authority or group standards of conduct and belief.

3️⃣ Unwarranted Assumptions and Stereotypes ❓

An assumption is something we believe to be true without any proof or conclusive evidence.

💡 Example: If the weather report calls for rain, we take an umbrella assuming the meteorologist is not lying, the report is scientific, and the instruments are accurate.

4️⃣ Relativistic Thinking 🌀

Relativism is the view that truth is a matter of opinion rather than objective reality. It is one of the strongest challenges to critical thinking.

  • • Subjectivism: Truth is purely a matter of individual opinion.
    💡 Example: If Abdella believes abortion is wrong and Obang believes it is not, subjectivism claims abortion is wrong for Abdella and right for Obang, and both are "true."
  • • Cultural Relativism: Truth is a matter of social or cultural opinion.
    💡 Example: Drinking wine is considered wrong in Iran but permissible in France; thus cultural relativism holds it is immoral in Iran but moral in France.
  • • Moral Relativism: Includes moral subjectivism and cultural moral relativism, holding that moral goodness is relative to individual or cultural belief.

5️⃣ Wishful Thinking 🌠

Believing something not because you have good evidence for it, but simply because you wish it were true. People fear the unknown and invent comforting myths to render the universe less hostile and more predictable.

🎓 Benefits of Critical Thinking

🎯 Critical Thinking: Skills and Dispositions

Critical thinking teaches how to:

  • ✅ Identify and raise fundamental questions and problems in the community clearly and precisely.
  • ✅ Gather and assess relevant information using abstract ideas.
  • ✅ Develop reasoned conclusions and solutions, testing them against relevant criteria and standards.
  • ✅ Remain open-minded to alternative systems of thought while recognizing your own assumptions.
🏫 Critical Thinking in the Classroom:
Critical thinking is what university is all about! University is not only about teaching facts; it's about teaching students how to think critically. Students improve classroom performance by:
  • • Understanding the arguments and beliefs of others.
  • • Critically evaluating those arguments and beliefs.
  • • Developing and defending one’s own well-supported arguments and beliefs.

🌟 Practical Life Benefits

  • • Helps us avoid making foolish personal decisions.
  • • Promotes democratic processes by enabling citizens to evaluate public policies and make informed electoral decisions.
  • • Fosters personal enrichment and self-improvement by breaking the common habit of blindly believing whatever we are told.
