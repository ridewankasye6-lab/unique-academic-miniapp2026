📖 CHAPTER THREE: LEARNING AND THEORIES OF LEARNING

💥 Definition, Characteristics, and Principles of Learning

📚 What is Learning?

"Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior occurring as a result of experience or practice."

Let's break this down 🔍:

  • ✅ Learning is a change in behavior
    • Imagine a child who once feared dogs but now plays joyfully with them after many friendly encounters. That's learning in action!
  • ✅ The change is relatively permanent
    • It's not just a one-time event – real learning tends to stick with you. For example, learning to ride a bike usually lasts a lifetime.
  • ✅ It results from experience or practice
    • You can't just learn by reading alone – you learn by doing! 🗣️💬 Practice makes perfect!
  • ✅ The learning is not directly observable
    • It manifests in the activities of the individual.

🌟 3.1.2. Characteristics of Learning

  • Learning is continuous modification of behavior throughout life.
  • Learning is pervasive, it reaches into all aspects of human life.
  • Learning involves the whole person, socially, emotionally & intellectually.
  • Learning is often a change in the organization of experiences.
  • Learning is responsive to incentives.
  • Learning is an active process.
  • Learning is purposeful.
  • Learning depends on maturation, motivation and practice.
  • Learning is multifaceted.

💡 3.1.3. Principles of Learning

Individuals learn best:

  • When they are physically, mentally, & emotionally ready.
  • When they have meaningful practice and exercise.
  • When it is accompanied by a pleasant or satisfying feeling.
  • Primacy: Things learned first create a strong unforgettable impression.
  • Recency: Things most recently learned are best remembered.
  • Intensity: The principle of intensity implies that a student will learn more from the real thing than from a substitute.
  • Freedom: Things freely learned are best learned – higher intellectual and moral advancement occur when learning is through freedom.

📊 3.2. Factors Influencing Learning

Key factors that directly affect how effectively an individual learns include:

  • Motivation
  • Maturation
  • Health condition of the learner
  • Psychological wellbeing of the learner
  • Good learning conditions
  • Background experiences
  • Length of the working period
  • Massed and distributed learning
💡 Reflection Question: Do learning materials, teaching styles, teaching methods, and medium of instruction affect learning like the factors listed above? Why?

🧩 3.3. Theories of Learning and Their Applications

1. Behavioral Theory of Learning

Behavioral theory of learning believes that learning occurs as a result of stimulus-response associations.

Behavioral theories emphasize observable behaviors, seek laws to govern all organisms, and provide explanations which focus on consequences.

There are two major behavioral theories of learning: Classical Conditioning and Operant Conditioning.

🧪 1 A. Pavlov's Classical Conditioning Theory

This is sometimes called learning through association 🧠💭.

💡 What does it mean?

Classical conditioning focuses on the learning of making involuntary emotional or physiological responses to stimuli that normally elicit no response.

👉 For example:
  • You see a hyena 🐺 ➔ Your heart races 💓, you sweat 😰, or you feel fear 😨

These responses (like fear or sweating) are automatic – you didn't consciously choose them.

🔄 How Does Classical Conditioning Work?

Through the process of classical conditioning, both humans and animals can be trained to act involuntarily to a stimulus that previously had no effect – or had a different effect.

✅ This is possible because classical conditioning involves conditioned reflexes (e.g., the production of saliva in response to food).

In short, classical conditioning (sometimes called substitution learning) is a type of learning in which a neutral stimulus comes to bring about a response after it is paired with a stimulus that naturally brings about that response.

⚙️ Basics of Classical Conditioning

To demonstrate classical conditioning, we must first identify stimuli and responses:

  • Neutral stimulus (NS): A stimulus that, before conditioning, does not naturally bring about the response of interest.
  • Unconditioned stimulus (UCS): A stimulus that naturally brings a particular response without having been learned.
  • Unconditioned response (UCR): A response that is natural and needs no training (e.g., salivation at the smell of food).
  • Conditioned stimulus (CS): A once neutral stimulus that has been paired with an UCS to bring about a response formerly caused only by the UCS.
  • Conditioned response (CR): A response to the CS.

📌 Principles of Classical Conditioning

  • A. Stimulus Generalization: A process in which stimuli that are similar to the original stimulus begin to produce the same responses.
    Example: A dog conditioned to salivate to a dinner bell (CS) might also salivate to a door bell or a telephone bell.
  • B. Stimulus Discrimination: The ability to differentiate similar stimuli and respond only to a specific stimulus.
    Example: The dog salivates only in response to the dinner bell instead of the doorbell or telephone bell.
  • C. Extinction: If a CS is repeatedly presented without presenting the UCS (meat), the CR will diminish and eventually stop occurring. Extinction only inhibits the CR; it does not eliminate it.
  • D. Spontaneous Recovery: The reemergence of an extinguished conditioned response after a period of rest and with no further conditioning.

⚙️ 1 B. Operant / Instrumental Conditioning

Operant conditioning (also called Instrumental conditioning) is learning in which a voluntary response is strengthened or weakened, depending on its consequences.

In operant conditioning, the organism's response operates or produces effects on the environment. These effects, in turn, influence whether the response will occur again.

Unlike classical conditioning, operant conditioning applies to voluntary responses, in which an organism performs deliberately to produce a desirable outcome.

🎯 Types of Consequences (Skinner)

According to B.F. Skinner, a response (operant) can lead to three types of consequences:

  • (a) Neutral Consequence: A consequence that does not alter the response.
  • (b) Reinforcement: Strengthens the response. A reinforcer is any event that increases the re-occurrence of a behavior.
    • Primary reinforcers: Food, water, light, stroking of the skin, and comfortable air temperature satisfy biological needs naturally without prior learning.
    • Secondary reinforcers: Reinforce behavior because of prior association with primary reinforcing stimuli (e.g., money, praise, applause, good grades, awards, gold stars).
  • (c) Punishment: A stimulus that weakens the response or makes it less likely to recur.

➕ Positive and Negative Reinforcement

  • Positive Reinforcement: Process whereby presentation of a stimulus makes behavior more likely to occur again.
  • Negative Reinforcement: Process whereby termination of an aversive (unpleasant) stimulus makes behavior more likely to occur again.
    💡 Example: If someone nags you all the time to study, but stops nagging when you comply, your studying is likely to increase because you avoid the nagging.

Negative reinforcement involves two forms of learning:

  • Escape Learning: Animals learn to make a response that terminates/stops an ongoing noxious or unpleasant stimulus.
  • Avoidance Learning: Learning to make a response to prevent or avoid exposure to a painful stimulus prior to its occurrence.

📅 Schedules of Reinforcement

Continuous reinforcement (reinforcing every time) aids rapid initial acquisition. Partial/intermittent reinforcement (reinforcing only some responses) makes behavior more resistant to extinction.

Schedule Type Description Effect on Behavior
Fixed-Ratio Reinforcement occurs after a fixed number of responses. Produces high rate of responding (used by employers to boost productivity).
Variable-Ratio Reinforcement occurs after a varying number of responses. Produces extremely high steady rates; highly resistant to extinction.
Fixed-Interval Reinforcement occurs only if a fixed amount of time has passed. Responses increase near the end of the time interval.
Variable-Interval Reinforcement occurs after varying amounts of time have passed. Produces slow, steady rates of response.

🚫 Punishment & Its Pros and Cons

Punishers can be primary (inherently painful like extreme heat/cold) or secondary (criticism, scolding, fines, bad grades).

  • Positive Punishment: An unpleasant stimulus occurs following a behavior to decrease it.
  • Negative Punishment: A pleasant stimulus is removed following a behavior to decrease it.

Factors for Punishment Effectiveness:

  • Immediacy: Punishment must follow immediately after the behavior.
  • Consistency: Inconsistent punishment intermittently reinforces the bad behavior.
  • Intensity: Severe punishment is generally more effective, though mild punishment works if immediate and consistent.
⚠️ Why Punishment Often Fails:
  • Administered inappropriately or mindlessly.
  • Recipient responds with anxiety, fear, or rage.
  • Effectiveness is often temporary (depends on presence of punisher).
  • Hard to punish immediately in real life.
  • Conveys little information about what the correct behavior should be.

✂️ Shaping

Shaping is an operant conditioning procedure in which successive approximations of a desired response are reinforced until the final target behavior is achieved.

🛠️ Applications of Behavioral Learning Theories

  • Conditioning study behavior
  • Conditioning classroom behavior
  • Managing problem behavior
  • Dealing with anxieties through conditioning
  • Conditioning group behavior
  • Conditioning and cognitive processes
  • Shaping complex behavior

👥 2. Social Learning Theory (Observational Learning)

According to Albert Bandura, human learning consists of observational learning – learning by watching the behavior of another person, or model. It is also called social cognitive learning.

🎁 Three Forms of Reinforcement in Observational Learning

  • Direct Reinforcement: The observer reproduces the model's behavior and receives direct rewards.
  • Vicarious Reinforcement: The observer sees the model being reinforced for a behavior and increases their own performance of that behavior.
  • Self-Reinforcement: Controlling one's own reinforcers; learners feel satisfaction and enjoy their growing competence without external rewards.
💡 Classic Study: Bandura's famous Bobo Doll Experiment demonstrated how children observe and imitate aggressive behaviors modeled by adults.

🧠 Essential Conditions for Modeling

  1. Attention: The learner must first pay attention to the model.
  2. Retention: Remembering the behavior that was observed.
  3. Motor Reproduction: Replicating and performing the action.
  4. Motivation: Having a motivating factor or reason to demonstrate what was learned.

🏫 Educational Implications of Social Learning Theory

  • Students learn simply by observing others.
  • Describing consequences involves discussing rewards and penalties with learners.
  • Modeling provides a faster, more efficient alternative to shaping.
  • Teachers and parents must model appropriate behaviors carefully.
  • Expose students to a variety of positive role models.
  • Help students develop self-efficacy and set realistic academic goals.
  • Utilize self-regulation techniques for improving behavior.

🧠 3. Cognitive Learning Theory

Cognition refers to all mental activities related to thinking and knowing, including perception and memory.

• Classical and operant conditioning depend on events closely linked in time and space (contiguity).

💡 Example: A bell 🔔 rings right before food 🍎 ➔ dog learns to salivate.

But cognitive theory says:

  • Learning can happen inside the mind without immediate external rewards or obvious connections.
  • This learning may not be visible at first.

💡 Two Key Forms of Cognitive Learning:

  1. Latent Learning 🕵️‍♂️
  2. Insight Learning 💡✨

🕵️‍♂️ 1. Latent Learning

What is it?

  • Learning that happens without obvious reinforcement or visible behavior changes.
  • The learning is hidden (latent) until a reason/reinforcement is introduced to reveal it.
🧪 Tolman & Honzik's Classic Experiment (1930):

Three groups of rats were placed in mazes over two weeks:

  • Rat-A: Always found food at the end. Quickly learned the maze without dead ends.
  • Rat-B: Never received food. Wandered without learning a direct path.
  • Rat-C: Received no food for 10 days, but received food on day 11. On day 12, Rat-C ran the maze as fast as Rat-A!

Conclusion: Rat-C had formed a mental cognitive map of the maze during the unrewarded days. Learning was latent until rewarded!

💡 2. Insight Learning (Gestalt / Perceptual Learning)

A cognitive process whereby we reorganize our perception of a problem. It does not depend on step-by-step conditioning of specific behaviors.

  • Occurs suddenly after a period where no apparent progress was made.
  • Solutions found through insight can easily be applied to other similar situations.
  • Accompanied by a satisfying "Aha!" experience 🎉.
