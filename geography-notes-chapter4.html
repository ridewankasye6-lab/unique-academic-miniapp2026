🌍 Geography Unit 4

Drainage Systems and Water Resources of Ethiopia and the Horn

🌐 4.1. Introduction

Global Water Distribution

Globally, water bodies occupy nearly ¾ (71%) of the earth's surface, mainly oceans and seas.

  • Nearly 97.5% is alkaline (found in oceans and seas).
  • Only about 2.5% is fresh water, out of which:
    • Nearly 68.7% is deposited in glaciers / ice mass.
    • 30.1% exists as ground water.
    • 0.8% exists in permafrost / permanently frozen land of polar areas.
    • 0.4% in surface waters (e.g., lakes, rivers, atmosphere, soil, and wetlands).
💡 Key Fact: Relatively, the most abundant and easily available fresh waters are surface water and ground water. Freshwater is unevenly distributed throughout the world based on latitudinal location, climate, and topography.

Ethiopia's Water Resource Potential

  • About 0.7% of Ethiopia's land mass is covered by water bodies.
  • The country has a larger volume of surface and ground water mainly due to:
    • The diverse topographical setup;
    • Relatively higher rainfall;
    • Nearness to the equator.
  • Ethiopia's surface water potential (e.g., lakes, rivers, atmosphere, soil, and wetlands) is estimated to be 124.4 billion cubic meters (BCM).
🏆 Title: Hence, Ethiopia is referred to as "the water tower of Eastern Africa".

🌊 4.2. Major Drainage System of Ethiopia

Core Concepts

  • Drainage: Conceptualized as the flow of water through a well-defined channel.
  • Drainage System: Consists of the channels through which a principal river and its tributaries flow.
  • River System: Begins at the source or headwater & ends at the mouth. Thus, a drainage system is a branched network of stream channels together with the adjacent land slopes they drain.
  • Drainage Basin: The topographic region from which a river & its tributaries collect both the surface runoff and subsurface flow. It is bounded by and separated from other river basins by a water divide or topographic divide.

Factors Influencing Drainage Patterns

The drainage pattern of an area is the result of:

  • The geological processes (faulting, folding);
  • Nature and structure of rocks (rough or smooth);
  • Topography (rugged or plain);
  • Slope (steep or gentle);
  • Amount and the periodicity (timing) of the flow.

Topographical Determinants in Ethiopia

In Ethiopia, the general patterns of major river basins are highly determined by topographical structures:

  • Outward sloping of the Western and Southeastern plateaus result in:
    • Western and Southeastern drainage systems
  • Inward sloping escarpments and the structural formation of the Rift Valley result in:
    • Rift Valley or inland drainage system
  • Faults structurally influence part of the course of some rivers.

🗺️ The Major Drainage Systems

The complex physiographic setup and geological makeup of Ethiopia result in three broadly classified drainage systems:

  1. Western Drainage Systems
  2. Southeastern Drainage Systems
  3. Rift Valley Drainage Systems

4.2.1. The Western Drainage Systems

  • They are the largest of all drainage systems;
  • Drain 40% of Ethiopia's landmass;
  • Carry 60% of the annual water flow of Ethiopia.
  • Most of the catchment extends to the westward sloping part of the western highlands and lowlands.
  • Consist of the basins of Rivers: Tekeze, Abay, Baro-Akobo, and Ghibe (Omo).
  • The Ghibe (Omo) flows southward to join Lake Turkana (an elongated Rift Valley Lake).
  • Others (Tekeze, Abay & Baro-Akobo) flow westward to join the White Nile and finally end at the Mediterranean Sea.
💧 Abay River Basin Focus:
  • Abay is the largest both in coverage & discharge volume from the western drainage system.
  • It has an area coverage of 199,812 km², with an estimated mean annual total flow volume of 45-50 BCM.
  • Carries (together with its tributaries) 65% of the annual water flow of the region.
  • Covers parts of Amhara, Oromia & Benishangul-Gumuz.
  • Rises from Lake Tana (Sekela, Chocke mountain) & flows for about 1,450 km; joins the White Nile in Khartoum (forming the Nile).
  • It first flows eastward, then turns 180° making a large bend flowing westward.
  • More than 60 streams drain the Abay within elevation ranging between 500-4261 m.a.s.l.
  • Ghilgel Abay (Little Abay) is the largest of its tributaries.

Major Drainage Systems Classification

The complex physiographic setup and geological makeup of Ethiopia result in three broadly classified drainage systems:

  1. Western Drainage Systems
  2. Southeastern Drainage Systems
  3. Rift Valley Drainage Systems

🏞️ 4.2.1. The Western Drainage Systems

  • They are the largest of all drainage systems;
  • Drain 40% of Ethiopia’s landmass, and
  • Carry 60% of the annual water flow of Ethiopia.
  • Most of the catchment extends to the westward sloping part of the western highlands and lowlands.
  • Consist of the basins of Rivers: Tekeze, Abay, Baro-Akobo, Ghibe (Omo).
  • The Ghibe (Omo) flows southward to join Lake Turkana (an elongated Rift Valley Lake);
  • Others (Tekeze, Abay & Baro-Akobo) flow westward to join White Nile and finally ends at Mediterranean sea.

Abay River Basin

  • Abay is the largest both in coverage & discharge volume from the western drainage system.
    • It has area coverage of 199,812 km²; with estimated mean annual total flow volume is 45-50 BCM.
    • Carries (together with its tributaries) 65% of the annual water flow of the region.
    • Covers parts of Amhara, Oromia & Benishangul-Gumuz.
    • Rises from Lake Tana (Sekela, Chocke mountain) & flows for about 1,450 km; joins White Nile in Khartoum (forming Nile).
    • It first flows eastward then turns 180° making a large bend flowing westward.
    • More than 60 streams drain the Abay within elevation ranging between 500-4261 m.a.s.l.
    • Ghilgel Abay (Little Abay) is the largest of its tributaries.

Tekeze and its tributaries

  • Carries 12% of the annual water flow of the region;
  • Drains 82,350 km² of land surface within elevation ranging between 536–4517 m.a.s.l.
  • Tekeze basin, rises in the central highlands and has two main tributaries: Angereb and Goang.
  • The total mean annual flow from the basin is about 8.2 BCM.
  • In Sudan, Tekeze is named as Atbara, which is the tributary of Nile River.

Baro-Akobo and Ghibe/Omo Rivers

  • The two rivers drain the wettest highlands in the South and Southwestern Ethiopia.
  • In the lower course, the Baro River flows across an extensive marshy land.
  • Baro together with Akobo forms the Sobat River in South Sudan.
  • Baro-Akobo basin carries 17% of the annual water flow and has an area of 75,912 km².
    • The total mean annual flow from the basin is estimated to be 23.6 BCM.
    • Covers parts of the Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambella, Oromia, and SNNPR.
  • Ghibe/Omo basin carries 6% of the annual water flow and drains an area of 79,000 km².
    • The total mean annual flow from the basin is estimated to be 16.6 BCM.

🏜️ 4.2.2. The Southeastern Drainage Systems

  • Drain nearly the entire southeastern physiographic region.
  • Comprises the outward sloping of the southeastern highlands.
  • It is mainly drained by Rivers Wabishebelle and Ghenale (plains of water deficient).
  • Wabishebelle and Ghenale rivers flow to Somalia, carrying 25% of Ethiopia's annual water flow.
  • Wabishebelle is the longest river in Ethiopia and has the largest catchment (202,697 km²).
    • It drains parts of Oromia, Harari and the Somali regions.
    • Most of its tributaries are intermittent.
    • Wabishebelle river disappears in the sands, just near Juba River.
  • Ghenale River basin has an area of 171,042 km², covering parts of Oromia, SNNPR, and Somali regions.
    • Ghenale, has fewer tributaries but carries more water than Wabishebelle, and so reaches the Indian Ocean.
    • Ghenale basin flows is estimated to be 5.8 BCM within elevation ranging between 171-4385 m.a.s.l.
    • In Somalia it is named the Juba River.

🌋 4.2.3. The Rift Valley Drainage Systems

The catchment area of this drainage system comprises the slopes of the escarpment and the Rift Valley floor.

The drainage system is characterized by:

  • Small catchment area,
  • Small amount of rainfall,
  • High evaporation.

Afar Drainage

  • Is a sub-basin which has no stream/river/flow.
  • Little rain, very high temperature and very high evaporation.
  • Here, the only main surface waters are Lakes Afrera & Asale.

Awash River

  • Is the only major river basin with catchment area of 114,123 km² and a mean annual discharge of 4.9 BCM.
  • Originates from Shewan plateau in the central highlands;
  • Flows 1250 km and covers parts of Amhara, Oromia, Afar, Somali, Dire Dawa and AA.
  • Is the most utilized river in Ethiopia.
  • Flows in a north east direction and finally ends in marshy area and small lakes (e.g. Lake Abe).

Lakes Region

  • Is the southern part of the Rift Valley sub-basin;
  • Characterized by many lakes that occupy the fault depression;
  • There are also small streams/rivers that drain from the nearby mountain slopes and supply water to the lakes:
    • Meki and Katar Rivers flow into Ziway;
    • Bilate flow into Abaya; and
    • Segen flow into Chew Bahir.
  • Lakes Ziway, Langano and Abijiata are hydrologically interconnected via Bulbula and Horacolo rivers:
    • Lake Ziway drains into Abijata through Bulbula stream/river.
    • Lake Langano drains into Lake Abijata through the Horacolo stream/river.

🌊 4.3. Water Resources: Rivers, Lakes (Surface) and Subsurface Water

4.3.1. Ethiopian Rivers

  • Ethiopia is endowed with many rivers.
  • Majority of them originate from highland areas and cross the Ethiopian boundary.
  • Ethiopian rivers form 12 major watersheds.

General Characteristics of Ethiopian Rivers

The characteristics of Ethiopian rivers is influenced by:

  • The highland nature of the Ethiopian landmass,
  • The surface ruggedness,
  • The outward inclination of the highlands, and
  • The climatic conditions (e.g., Rainfall seasonality).
📌 Overall Characteristics:
  • Almost all major rivers originate from the highlands elevating more than 1500 m.a.s.l.
  • Majority of Ethiopian rivers are trans-boundary.
  • They have rapids and waterfalls along their course, due to surface ruggedness.
  • They have cuts/dissections, steep-sided river valleys and deep gorges along their courses.
  • Ethiopian rivers are also characterized by extreme seasonal fluctuation due to the clear seasonality of rainfall:
    • In the wet seasons, there is higher runoff and rivers get full bursting their banks, destroying small bridges, damage roads and flooding low lands;
    • During the dry seasons they become mere trickles/scarce of water or even some of them dry up.
  • They flow on steep slopes having steep profiles.
  • Some of the rivers serve as boundaries, both international and domestic administrative units.

⛵ 4.3.2. Ethiopian Lakes

  • Ethiopia is rich in lakes compared to other countries.
  • Almost all Ethiopian lakes are the result of tectonic processes that took place during Quaternary period of Cenozoic era.
  • Majority of lakes are located within the Rift Valley System & are mainly formed on faulted depressions; clustered and lined up.
  • From the lakes in the Central Ethiopian Rift:
    • The largest is Lake Abaya;
    • The deepest is Lake Shala, and
    • The shallowest is Lake Ziway.
  • In the southern tip of the Rift Valley there is a marshy land - the Chew Bahir which is drained by Segan and Woito Rivers.
  • The largest lake in Ethiopia is Lake Tana, which is a highland lake.
    • The lake is believed to have been formed following slower sinking of lava flow b/n Gojjam and Gonder massifs.
  • Lake Ashenge (Tigray) is also formed on a tectonic basin.
  • In Ethiopia there are also many crater lakes such as:
    • Lakes at and around Bishoftu, Wonchi (near Ambo), Hayk (near Dessie) and lake on mount Zikwala.
  • Man-made lakes include Lakes Koka, Fincha and Melka Wakena.

🕳️ 4.3.3. Subsurface (Ground) Water Resource of Ethiopia

  • Ethiopia has lower potential of ground water compared to surface water resources (124.4 BCM).
  • But there is higher total exploitable groundwater potential.
  • The availability of groundwater is determined by:
    • Climate and geophysical conditions.
  • The groundwater potential of Ethiopia was previously estimated to be 2.6 - 6.5 BCM.
    • This figure is now considered underestimated.
  • Based on various separate studies, Ethiopia’s groundwater potential is expected to be between 12-30 BCM.

⚡ 4.4. Water Resources Potentials and Development in Ethiopia

Despite Ethiopia’s huge water resource potential, this potential is underutilized due to many factors: seasonality, variability, sedimentation, rapids and waterfalls, invasive species.

However, there are ample opportunities to transform Ethiopia’s water resources into different development uses including:

a) Hydro-electric Potential

  • Ethiopia’s rivers have very high potential for electricity power generation.
  • The estimated exploitable potential of hydroelectric power is about 45,000 megawatts (MW).
  • In Ethiopia, the first hydroelectric power generation plant - Aba Samuel - was installed in 1932, on Akaki River.
  • Currently Ethiopia is administering 14 hydroelectric power plants, generating close to 4000 megawatts of energy.
    • These plants are constructed on Lake Aba Samuel, Koka, Tis Abay, Awash, Melka Wakena, Sor, Fincha, Gibe/Omo, Tana Beles and Tekeze.
  • Gilgel Gibe III hydropower project has gone operational generating 1870 megawatts.
  • Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) is Ethiopia’s largest dam under construction aiming to generate 6400 megawatts.
  • Recently initiated is Gilgel Gibe IV (Koysha) – 2160 megawatts.
  • Majority of hydro-electric reservoirs have multi-purpose (water supply, irrigation, fisheries, navigation, recreational activities etc).
⚠️ Major Challenges for Hydroelectric Power:
  • Seasonal flow fluctuations & impact of climate change & variability.
  • Severe erosion from the highlands and sedimentation in the reservoirs.

b) Irrigation and Transportation

The uses of Ethiopian rivers both for irrigation and transportation is hindered by the rugged topography (steep slopes, rapids, waterfalls, narrow & deep valleys & gorges).

Irrigation

  • Ethiopia’s potential of irrigation is estimated to be 5.3 million hectares.
  • The two rivers with large irrigation potential are the Baro-Akobo and Genale Dawa.
  • Of the total irrigated area of Ethiopia, more than 60% is located in the Rift Valley Drainage System.

Transportation

  • Majority of Ethiopian rivers are not suitable for transportation.
  • The only navigable river is Baro at its lower course.
  • Ethiopian lakes are much suitable for transportation than rivers.
  • Lake Tana and Lake Abaya are relatively the most used for transportation.

c) Fishing and Recreation

Fishing

  • Most of Ethiopian lakes are rich in fish resource.
  • The estimated annual fish production is 31.5 thousand tons.
  • However, the exploitable potential is by far greater than the current production.
  • Ethiopia’s Lakes vary in their exploitable fish potential:
    • Lake Tana has the highest potential, estimated 8,000-10,000 tons/year.
    • Lake Chamo’s potential is estimated at 4,500 tons/year.
  • More than 60% of fish supplies is from main Rift Valley lakes.
⚠️ Threats to Ethiopian Rift Valley Lakes:
  • Sedimentation
  • Invasive species (e.g., Water hyacinth)
  • Over exploitation (of water, fish and other resources of the lakes)
  • Expansion of investments around lakes (e.g., Flower farm around Lake Ziway)

Recreation

Most of Ethiopian rivers and lakes are important recreational and tourist attraction areas because of:

  • The variety of fish, birds and other aquatic life forms in the lakes:
    • e.g., White Pelicans and Pink Flamingos (Lake Abijata and Shala); Ostrich
  • The natural scenery/beauty of the lakes,
  • The hot springs around them,
  • The spectacular river gorges and the most impressive waterfalls.
  • Habitats of wildlife (some endemic):
    • Hippos (Lake Tana, Chamo and Hawassa)
    • Crocodiles (Lake Chamo)
