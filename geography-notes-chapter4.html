Drainage Systems and Water Resources of Ethiopia and the Horn 💧

🌊 Introduction to Water Resources

  • About 71% of the earth’s total surface is covered by water bodies majorly occupied by seas and oceans, which accounts for about (97.5%), alkaline.
  • The remaining 2.5% is fresh water, of which nearly:
    • 68.7% is deposited in glaciers 🧊
    • 30.1% in ground water 🕳️
    • 0.8% in permafrost ❄️
    • 0.4% in surface waters (Lakes, rivers, atmosphere, soils and wetlands) 🌧️
  • Surface and ground waters are the most abundant and easily available fresh waters. 💧
  • However, fresh water is distributed unevenly throughout the world following varied latitudinal locations, climatic and topographic setups. 🌐
📍 Ethiopia's Topographic & Hydrological Setup:
  • As you have discussed in the third chapter, the topographic setup of Ethiopia is characterized by a complex blend of massive highlands, rugged terrain, and low plains. ⛰️
  • The diverse topographical setup, relatively higher rainfall, and its nearness to the equator made the country have a larger volume of ground and surface water. 🌧️
  • Around 0.7% of the total land mass of Ethiopia is covered by water bodies. 🏞️
  • Although it requires further detailed investigation, the country’s surface water potential as studied in different integrated river basin master plans is estimated to be 124.4 billion cubic meters (BCM). 📊
  • Consequently, many call Ethiopia the “Water Tower of Eastern Africa”. 🏰💧

🗺️ Major Drainage System of Ethiopia

📖 Definition: The flow of water through a well-defined channel is known as drainage.
  • A drainage system is made up of a principal river and its tributaries (the rivers that flow into it). 🌿
  • It begins at a place called the source or headwater 🏔️ and ends at a point called the mouth 🌊.
  • Therefore, a drainage system is a branched network of stream channels together with the adjacent land slopes they drain. 🌿
  • The drainage pattern of an area is the outcome of geological processes, the nature and structure of rocks, topography, slope, amount, and the periodicity of the flow. 🪨
  • A drainage basin is the topographic region from which a river and its tributaries collect both the surface runoff and subsurface flow. 🏞️
  • It is bounded by and separated from other river basins by a water divide or topographic divide. ⛰️

🧭 General Patterns of Major River Basins

The general patterns of major river basins in Ethiopia are determined by topographical structures and can be classified as:

  1. The outward sloping of the Western plateaus 🏔️
  2. South eastern plateaus 🌄
  3. The structural formation of the Rift Valley with its inward-sloping escarpments resulting mainly in an inland drainage system 🌋

⚡ Faults and joints structurally influence part of the courses of many rivers.

🏞️ The Major Drainage Systems Overview

Ethiopia possesses three broadly classified drainage systems, namely:

  1. The Western Drainage System ⬅️
  2. The Southeastern Drainage System ↘️
  3. The Rift Valley Drainage System 🌋
💡 Key Structural Note: The Western and Southeastern drainage systems are separated by the Rift Valley system.

⬅️ 1. The Western Drainage Systems

  • The largest of all drainage systems, draining 40% of the total area and carrying 60% of the annual water flow. 🌊
  • Most of the catchment area coextends with the westward sloping part of the western highlands and western lowlands. 🏔️
  • This drainage system comprises four major river basins:
    • The Tekeze 🌊
    • The Abay 🌊
    • The Baro-Akobo 🌊
    • The Ghibe (Omo) 🌊
  • ⚠️ Unlike other river basins in the system, the Ghibe (Omo) flows southward. ⬇️
  • The Abay, Tekeze, and Baro flow westward ultimately joining the Nile, which finally ends at the Mediterranean Sea. 🌊🇪🇬

🌊 Abay River Basin

  • The largest river both in volumetric discharge and coverage in the western drainage systems is the Abay. 🏆
  • Abay river basin covers an area of 199,812 km², covering parts of Amhara, Oromia, and Benishangul-Gumuz regional states. 📍
  • Together with its tributaries, most of which are left-bank tributaries, it carries 65% of the annual water flow of the region. 💧
  • Abay, which rises from Lake Tana (some sources indicate its origin from Sekela, Choke mountain), flows about 1,450 km and joins the White Nile in Khartoum, Sudan to form the Nile River. 🤝
  • More than 60 streams drain the Abay within elevations ranging between 500 – 4261 masl.
  • The largest of these streams is Ghilgel Abay (Little Abay).
  • Abay flows eastward, turns 180° to make a large bend, and after cutting an impressive and deep gorge, emerges out in the west. 🌀

🌊 Tekeze River Basin

  • The Tekeze and its tributaries, carrying 12% of the annual water flow of the region, drain 82,350 km² of land surface within elevations ranging between 536 - 4517 masl. 🏔️
  • Erosion in the basin resulted in large tablelands, plateau blocks, and isolated mountain groups. 🪨
  • The basin has two main tributaries: Angereb and Goang, which rise in the central highlands of Ethiopia. 🏞️
  • Tekeze River is termed Atbara in Sudan, which is a tributary of the Nile. 🇸🇩
  • The total mean annual flow from the basin is estimated to be 8.2 billion metric cubes (BMC). 📊

🌊 Baro-Akobo & Ghibe/Omo River Basins

  • The Baro-Akobo and Ghibe/Omo rivers drain the wettest highlands in south and southwestern Ethiopia. 🌧️
  • They carry 17% and 6% of the annual water flow, respectively. 💧
  • Baro-Akobo River Basin:
    • Has an area of 75,912 km², covering parts of Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambella, Oromia, and SNNPR. 📍
    • Total mean annual flow is estimated to be 23.6 BMC. 📊
    • In its lower course, the Baro River flows across an extensive marshy land. 🌾
    • The Baro together with Akobo forms the Sobat River in South Sudan. 🇸🇸
  • Ghibe / Omo River Basin:
    • Drains an area of 79,000 km² with an estimated mean annual flow of 16.6 BMC. 📊
    • The Ghibe / Omo River finally empties into the Chew-Bahir at the mouth of Lake Turkana (an elongated Rift Valley lake), thereby forming an inland drainage. 🏞️

↘️ II. The Southeastern Drainage Systems

  • Nearly the entire physiographic region of the southeastern part of Ethiopia is drained by the southeastern drainage systems. 🌄
  • The basin, which is mainly drained by Wabishebelle and Ghenale, slopes southeastwards across large water-deficient plains. 🏜️
  • Major highlands of this basin include the plateaus of Arsi, Bale, Sidama, and Harerghe. 🏔️
  • Wabishebelle and Ghenale rivers cross the border into Somalia, carrying 25% of the annual water flow of Ethiopia. 🇸🇴

🌊 Wabishebelle River Basin

  • With a total catchment area of 202,697 km², Wabishebelle is the largest river in terms of catchment area. 📏
  • It drains parts of Oromia, Harari, and the Somali regions. 📍
  • It is the longest river in Ethiopia. 🏁
  • Its tributaries are mainly left-bank, and most of them are intermittent. ⏳
  • ⚠️ Despite its size, the Wabishebelle fails to reach the Indian Ocean; at the end of its journey, it flows parallel to the coast before its water disappears in the sands, just near the Juba River. ⏳🏜️

🌊 Ghenale River Basin

  • Ghenale River basin has an area of 171,042 km², covering parts of Oromia, SNNPR, and Somali regions. 📍
  • Ghenale has fewer tributaries but carries more water than Wabishebelle, and successfully reaches the Indian Ocean. 🌊🏖️
  • The basin flow is estimated to be 5.8 BMC within elevations ranging between 171 - 4385 masl. 📊
  • In Somalia, it is named the Juba River. 🇸🇴

🌋 III. The Rift Valley Drainage System

This drainage system is an area characterized by a small amount of rainfall, high evaporation rates, and a small catchment area. ☀️

  • The size of the drainage area is restricted by the outward sloping highlands, which start right from the edge of the escarpment. ⛰️
  • The Rift Valley drainage system is therefore left with the slopes of the escarpment and the Rift Valley floor itself as its catchment area. 🏞️
  • In the Rift Valley drainage systems, there is no one general flow direction, as the streams flow in all directions. 🧭

🌊 Awash River Basin

  • The only major river basin in this system is that of the Awash. 🌊
  • Awash river basin has a catchment area of 114,123 km² and has an average annual discharge of 4.9 billion cubic meters (BCM). 📊
  • The Awash River originates from the Shewan plateau in the central highlands of Ethiopia and flows 1,250 km. 📍
  • It covers parts of the Amhara, Oromia, Afar, Somali, Dire Dawa, and Addis Ababa City Administration. 🗺️
  • 🏆 Awash is the most utilized river in the country.
  • Following the Rift Valley orientation, the Awash flows in a northeast direction. ↗️
  • It finally ends in a maze of small lakes and marshy areas; the largest of which is Lake Abe on the Ethio-Djibouti border. 🇩🇯

🏜️ Afar Drainage Sub-Basin

  • The Afar drainage sub-basin has practically no stream flow. 🚫
  • It is an area of little rain, very high temperatures, and very high evaporation. 🌡️☀️
  • Lake Afrera and Lake Asale are the only main surface waters in the basin which are not the result of any meaningful surface flow. 💧
  • Their formation is strictly related to tectonic activities. 🌋

🏞️ Southern Rift Valley Lakes Region

  • The Southern part of the Rift Valley sub-basin is characterized by a number of lakes and small streams. 🌊
  • It is described as the lakes region, and these lakes occupy fault depressions. 🕳️
  • Small streams drain down from nearby mountain slopes to supply water to the lakes:
💡 Stream & Lake Inflows:
  • Meki and Katar Rivers flow into 👉 Lake Ziway
  • Bilate River flows into 👉 Lake Abaya
  • Segen River flows into 👉 Chew Bahir
🔗 Interconnected Lakes:

Lake Ziway and Lake Langano drain into Lake Abijata through the small streams of Bulbula and Horocolo respectively. 🌊

💧 Water Resources: Rivers, Lakes, and Sub-Surface Water

🌊 The Ethiopian Rivers

  • Unlike many other African countries, Ethiopia is endowed with many rivers, where the majority originate from highland areas and cross the Ethiopian boundary. 🌍
  • Altogether, Ethiopian rivers form 12 major watersheds separating the Mediterranean Sea from the Indian Ocean drainage systems. 🧭

📝 General Characteristics of Ethiopian Rivers

Owing to the highland nature of the Ethiopian landmass, surface ruggedness, the outward inclination of the highlands, and climatic conditions, Ethiopian rivers have the following characteristics:

  1. Highland Origin: Almost all major rivers originate from the highlands elevating more than 1,500 meters above sea level. 🏔️
  2. Trans-Boundary Nature: The majority of Ethiopian rivers are trans-boundary. 🌐
  3. Extreme Seasonal Fluctuations: Due to the marked seasonality of rainfall, in the wet season, runoff is higher and rivers burst their banks (destroying small bridges, damaging roads, and flooding lowlands); during dry seasons, they become mere trickles of water or even dry up completely. 🌧️🍂
  4. Rapids & Waterfalls: Due to surface ruggedness, they feature rapids and waterfalls along their course. 🌊⚡
  5. Deep Gorges: They have deep cuts, steep-sided river valleys, and deep gorges along their courses. ⛰️
  6. Steep Profiles: Rivers in Ethiopia flow on steep slopes having steep profiles. 📈
  7. Administrative Boundaries: Some rivers serve as natural boundaries for both international borders and domestic administrative units. 🗺️

🏞️ The Ethiopian Lakes

  • Relatively, Ethiopia is rich in lakes. 🌊
  • Almost all Ethiopian lakes are the result of tectonic processes that took place during the Quaternary period of the Cenozoic era. 🌋
  • Except for a few, the majority of lakes are located within the Rift Valley System. They are formed on faulted depressions and are clustered forming a linear pattern. 📍
  • Lake Tana, the largest lake in Ethiopia, occupies a shallow depression in the highlands. 🏆
  • The Tana depression is believed to be formed following slower sinking and damming by lava flow between the Gojjam and Gonder massifs. 🌋
  • Ethiopia is also gifted with crater lakes:
    • Lakes at and around Bishoftu 🌊
    • Lake Wonchi (near Ambo) 🌋
    • Lake Hayk (near Dessie) 🏞️
    • The Crater Lake on top of Mount Zikwala ⛰️
  • Lake Ashenge (Tigray) is formed on a tectonic basin. 🕳️
  • Man-made Lakes: Lakes dammed following hydroelectric power generation projects include Koka, Fincha, and Melka Wakena. 🏗️⚡
📍 Rift Valley Lakes Features:
  • Clusters of lakes are lined up within the main Ethiopian rift. 🔗
  • Lake Abaya is the largest of all lakes within the Rift Valley system. 📏
  • The southern tip forms the marshy land called Chew Bahir, which is drained by the Segan and Woito rivers. 🌾
  • Shala and Ziway are the deepest and shallowest lakes in the central Ethiopian Rift, respectively. ⚓

🕳️ Subsurface (Groundwater) & Development Potentials

💧 Subsurface (Ground) Water Potential

  • As compared to surface water resources, Ethiopia has a lower groundwater potential; however, there exists a higher total exploitable potential. 🕳️
  • Climatic and geophysical conditions determine the availability of groundwater resources. ⛅
  • Based on previous scanty knowledge, the groundwater potential was estimated at 2.6 - 6.5 BMC. 📊
  • However, recent studies estimate Ethiopia's actual groundwater potential to range between 12 - 30 BMC. 📈

⚡ A) Hydro-Electric Potential

  • Ethiopian rivers have a very high potential for generating electricity. ⚡
  • The total exploitable potential of hydroelectric power is estimated at about 45,000 megawatts (MW). 🔋
  • The first hydroelectric power plant was installed on the Akaki River (Aba Samuel) in 1932. 📜
  • The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) is the country's largest dam, aiming to generate 6,400 MW. 🏗️💧
  • The Gilgel Gibe III project generated 1,870 MW upon going operational. ⚡
  • Currently, Ethiopia administers 14 hydroelectric power plants constructed on Aba Samuel, Koka, Tis Abay, Awash, Melka Wakena, Sor, Fincha, Gibe/Omo, Tana Beles, and Tekeze, generating close to 4,000 MW. 🔌
  • Besides domestic consumption, Ethiopia exports electricity to neighboring countries. 🌍
⚠️ Major Challenges to River Utilization:
  1. Extreme seasonal flow fluctuations & climate change impacts 🍂
  2. Severe erosion from the highlands ⛰️
  3. Sedimentation in dams and reservoirs 🪵

🌾 B) Irrigation and Transportation

  • Irrigation: Ethiopia's potential for irrigation is estimated at 5.3 million hectares. 🌾
  • The Baro-Akobo and Genale-Dawa river systems have the largest irrigation potential. 💧
  • More than 60% of the currently irrigated area is located in the Rift Valley Drainage System. 🚜
  • Terrain Obstacles: Rugged terrain, steep slopes, rapids, waterfalls, and deep gorges hinder irrigation and river transport in highlands. ⛰️
  • Navigation: Baro River at its lower course is the only navigable river in Ethiopia. 🚢
  • Lakes are much better suited for transport; Lake Tana and Lake Abaya are the most utilized. 🛥️

🎣 C) Fishing and Recreation

  • Annual fish production is estimated at 31.5 thousand tons per year. 🐟
  • Lake Tana leads potential production with 8,000–10,000 tons/year, followed by Lake Chamo at 4,500 tons/year. 🎣
  • More than 60% of total fish supplies come from main Rift Valley lakes. 🌊
  • ⚠️ Threats to lakes: Sedimentation, invasive species (water hyacinth 🌿), over-exploitation, and expanding investments.
  • Recreation & Tourism: Scenic beauty, endemic wildlife, hot springs, river gorges, and waterfalls make water bodies major tourist and scientific hubs. 🦅🏖️
