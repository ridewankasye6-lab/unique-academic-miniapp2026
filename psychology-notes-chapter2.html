🔍 2.1 Meanings of Sensation and Perception

Let's start by reflecting on these common phrases:

“I heard but I didn’t listen”

“I touched but I didn’t grasp”

These statements reveal the difference between simply detecting a stimulus and actually understanding or interpreting it.

🔍 What is Sensation?

Sensation is the raw data stage of experience. It is the process through which sense organs detect stimulus from the environment and the receptor cells in the sense organs recode the physical energy or stimulations into a neural message (a phenomenon called transduction) and send the nerve impulses to the brain.

  • Your sense organs (eyes, ears, nose, skin, tongue) detect stimuli from the environment.
  • These stimuli (like light, sound, smell, etc.) are converted into neural signals through a process called transduction.
  • These signals are then sent to the brain for further processing.

🧠 What is Perception?

Perception is the process that organizes sensations into meaningful patterns. It is the process whereby the brain interprets sensations, giving them order and meaning.

💡 Key Distinction: Hearing sounds and seeing colors is largely a sensory process, but forming a melody and detecting patterns and shapes is largely a perceptual process.

⚡ 2.2 The Sensory Laws

To understand how sensation works, we study two key general laws of sensation:

  1. Sensory Thresholds: These are the limits of what we can detect.
  2. Sensory Adaptation: This explains why we stop noticing some things over time.

🚦 Sensory Threshold

How intense must a stimulus be before we notice it? This is where thresholds come in. Sensory threshold is the minimum point of intensity a sound or stimulus can be detected. There are two laws of sensory threshold:

🔹 Absolute Threshold (a.k.a. Limen)

This is the minimum level of stimulation that can be detected.

Formal Definition: The lowest intensity of a stimulus that a person can detect 50% of the time when presented again and again.

💡 Examples of Absolute Threshold:
  • A cup of coffee requires at least some sugar before you can say it tastes sweet. ☕🍬
  • In a dark room, the faintest light you can see is your absolute threshold for vision.

In addition to the intensity of the stimulus, absolute threshold is also affected by physical and psychological factors of the individual such as response-bias.

🔹 Difference Threshold

Difference threshold or Just Noticeable Difference (JND) is the minimum amount of change in the intensity/amount of a stimulus needed for us to recognize that a change has occurred.

💡 Example: A cup of coffee requires a certain additional amount of sugar in a cup of coffee to detect an increase in its sweetness.

Like in absolute threshold, the difference threshold for a particular sensory experience varies from person to person and from occasion to occasion. Psychologists formally define the difference threshold as the minimum change in stimulation that can be detected 50 percent of the time by a given person.

🌿 Sensory Adaptation

Your senses are constantly bombarded by stimulation, but why do you notice only certain stimuli?

Sensory adaptation is a tendency of our sensory receptors to have decreasing responsiveness to unchanging stimulus. It lets you detect potentially important changes in your environment while ignoring unchanging aspects of it.

💡 Local Example: People living in "Kera" / Addis Ababa stopped noticing the bad smell of that environment over time.
⚠️ Important Exceptions:
  • We may not adapt to extremely intense sensations.
  • Adapting to harmful stimuli might be harmful or even fatal.

🧩 2.3 Perception & Perceptual Processes

Perception is a meaning-making process. The major characteristics of the perceptual process include:

  • Selectivity of perception (attention)
  • Form perception
  • Depth perception
  • Perceptual constancy
  • Perceptual illusion

🎯 1. Selectivity of Perception (Attention)

Refers to the perceptual process that selects certain inputs for inclusion in your conscious experience, or awareness, at any given time, ignoring others.

The selectivity of perception implies that our field of experience is divided into what is known as Focus & Margin:

  • Focus: Events or stimuli that you perceive clearly are the focus of your experience.
  • Margin: Items or stimuli that you perceive dimly or vaguely are in the margin of your attention.

Attention shifts constantly. What is in the focus of your attention one moment may enter the margin, and what is in the margin may become your focus.

⚙️ Factors Determining Attention

Paying attention is generally a function of two factors:

A. External Factors (Characteristics of Stimuli)

These are characteristics generally found in the objects or stimuli to be perceived:

  • Size and Intensity: Bigger and brighter stimuli get attention.
  • Repetition: Frequently occurring stimuli attract focus.
  • Novelty: New or unusual stimuli stand out.
  • Movement: Moving objects are quickly noticed.

B. Internal Factors (Observer's Psychological State)

Even when a stimulus is bigger, brighter, new, frequent, or moving, you may not pay attention if you are not psychologically ready. Internal factors include:

  • Set or Expectancy: What you are prepared or expecting to perceive.
  • Motives or Needs: Personal drives or urgent physical/psychological requirements.

🎨 Form Perception

Visual sensations provide raw materials that are organized into meaningful patterns, shapes, forms, and concepts. The meaningful shapes or patterns made out of pieces of sensations refer to form perception.

To perceive forms, you need to distinguish a figure (an object) from its ground (its surrounding).

🔍 How We Separate Figure from Ground

1. Contours in Form Perception

You are able to separate forms from the general ground only because you can perceive contours. Contours are formed whenever a marked difference occurs in the brightness or color of the background.

2. Organization in Form Perception

When several objects are present in the visual field, we tend to perceive them as organized into patterns or groupings. As Gestalt psychologists stated: "The whole is more than the sum of its parts."

📐 Laws of Perceptual Organization (Gestalt Laws)

1️⃣ Law of Proximity 📍

Items that are close together in space or time tend to be perceived as belonging together or forming an organized group.

💡 Example: Dots spaced close together look like a cluster, not separate points.

2️⃣ Law of Similarity 🥑

Things that look alike (in color, shape, size) are grouped together.

💡 Example: Three dots arranged in a circle may be seen as a triangle because your brain groups the similar dots together.

Note: Similarity sometimes competes with other organizing principles like symmetry or good figure.

3️⃣ Law of Good Figure (Simplicity) 🔷

We tend to organize shapes into the simplest, most balanced, or symmetrical form that includes all parts.

💡 Example: Six dots arranged in a circle might be seen as a hexagon or star, rather than random dots.

4️⃣ Law of Continuity ➰

Your brain prefers to see smooth, continuous lines or curves rather than abrupt changes.

💡 Example: A line that starts out as a curve is seen as continuing on a smoothly curved course, not as broken parts.

5️⃣ Law of Closure 🔒

Perceptual processes organize the perceived world as complete by filling in gaps in stimulation to see a full image.

6️⃣ Law of Common Fate 🕊️

Objects that move together, or seem to move together in the same direction, are grouped as part of the same entity.

💡 Example: Flocks of birds, herds of cattle, or kids playing together moving in one direction are seen as one group.

🏔️ Depth Perception

While form perception is our understanding of the world in two dimensions (height & width), depth perception is our judgment of the world in the third dimension (distance or depth of objects).

Depth perception depends on binocular cues (requiring two eyes) and monocular cues (requiring one eye).

👀 Binocular Cues

Require the interaction of both eyes:

  • Retinal Disparity: The degree of difference between the images focused on the two retinas. The closer the object, the greater the retinal disparity.
  • Convergence: The degree to which the eyes turn inward to focus on an object. The closer the object, the greater the convergence.

👁️ Monocular Cues

Cues available to each eye alone (people with sight in one eye can still have good depth perception with these):

  • Accommodation: Change in the shape of the lens to focus an image on the retina. The closer the object, the greater the accommodation. Prolonged accommodation can alter depth perception.
  • Motion Parallax: The apparent motion of objects viewed during travel. Objects closer than the point of fixation appear to move backward quickly, while objects farther away appear to move slower or forward. Extremely distant objects (like the moon) appear to move forward with you.

🎨 Pictorial Cues

Cues artists use to create depth in drawings and paintings:

Pictorial Cue How It Works
Interposition (Overlap) An object that overlaps another object appears closer.
Relative Size If two objects are known to be equal in size, the one casting a smaller retinal image is perceived as farther away.
Linear Perspective Parallel lines appear to converge as they recede into the distance.
Elevation Objects placed higher in your visual field seem farther away on a canvas.
Shading Patterns Shadowed areas recede into the distance, while illuminated areas stand out.
Aerial Perspective Closer objects appear clearer and sharper than hazy, distant ones.
Texture Gradient The nearer an object, the more visual details we can make out; farther objects lose detail.

📏 Perceptual Constancies & Illusions

🚪 Perceptual Constancies

The image of a given object on your retina varies in size, shape, and brightness. Yet we continue to perceive the object as stable because of perceptual constancy.

  • Size Constancy: Interprets changes in retinal image size as changes in distance rather than actual physical size changes.
  • Shape Constancy: Assures that an object of known shape appears to maintain its normal shape regardless of viewing angle (e.g., an opening door forming a trapezoid on the retina is still recognized as a rectangle).
  • Brightness Constancy: We perceive an object as having constant brightness despite variations in reflected light intensity.

🔮 Perceptual Illusions & Misperceptions

  • Visual/Optical Illusions: Physical stimuli that consistently produce errors in perception (e.g., Moon illusion, Müller-Lyer illusion).
  • Hallucinations: Sensory experiences and perceptions that occur without corresponding external reality (visual or auditory).
  • Delusions: Unfounded, false beliefs strongly held despite clear lack of evidence:
    • Delusion of Persecution: Belief that dangerous enemies are out to harm/persecute you.
    • Delusion of Grandeur: Belief that you are unusually important or a special messenger.
    • Delusion of Reference: Tendency to interpret random outside messages as directly meant for yourself.

🔮 Extra Sensory Perception (ESP) / Paranormal Ability

ESP is perception that occurs independently of known sensory processes:

  • Telepathy: Thought transference between individuals / mind reading.
  • Clairvoyance: Supernormal awareness of distant events or objects without physical presence.
  • Precognition: The ability to see or predict future events.
  • Mediumship: Communicating with the spiritual world and the deceased.
  • Clairgustance: The paranormal ability to taste a substance without putting it in the mouth.
