🧠 2.1 Meanings of Sensation and Perception

What do these statements suggest to you?

  • "I heard but I didn't listen"
  • "I touched but I didn't grasp"

These statements reveal the difference between simply detecting a stimulus and actually understanding or interpreting it.

What is Sensation?

Sensation is the process through which sense organs detect stimulus from the environment and the receptor cells in the sense organs recode the physical energy or stimulations into a neural message—a phenomenon called transduction—and send the nerve impulses to the brain.

It represents the raw data stage of experience:

  • Your sense organs (eyes, ears, nose, skin, tongue) detect stimuli from the environment.
  • These stimuli (like light, sound, smell, etc.) are converted into neural signals through a process called transduction.
  • These signals are then sent to the brain for further processing.

What is Perception?

Perception is the process that organizes sensations into meaningful patterns. It is the process whereby the brain interprets sensations, giving them order and meaning.

💡 Comparison Example:
Hearing sounds and seeing colors is largely a sensory process, but forming a melody and detecting patterns and shapes is largely a perceptual process.

⚡ 2.2 The Sensory Laws

Sensory threshold and sensory adaptation are the two general laws of sensation that explain how sensation works.

1. Sensory Threshold

Sensory threshold is the minimum point of intensity a sound or stimulus can be detected. There are two laws of sensory threshold:

A. Absolute Threshold (Limen):

  • The minimum level of stimulation that can be detected.
  • In addition to the intensity of the stimulus, absolute threshold is affected by physical and psychological factors of the individual, such as response-bias.
  • Psychologists define absolute threshold as the minimum level of stimulation that can be detected 50% of the time when a stimulus is presented again and again.
💡 Example: A cup of coffee requires a certain amount of sugar before you could detect a sweet taste.

B. Difference Threshold (Just Noticeable Difference / JND):

  • The minimum amount of change in the intensity/amount of a stimulus needed for us to recognize that a change has occurred.
  • Like absolute threshold, the difference threshold for a particular sensory experience varies from person to person and from occasion to occasion.
  • Psychologists formally define difference threshold as the minimum change in stimulation that can be detected 50 percent of the time by a given person.
💡 Example: A cup of coffee requires a certain additional amount of sugar to detect an increase in its sweetness.

2. Sensory Adaptation

Sensory adaptation is a tendency of our sensory receptors to have decreasing responsiveness to an unchanging stimulus.

  • It lets you detect potentially important changes in your environment while ignoring unchanging aspects of it.
  • Limitations:
    • We may not adapt to extremely intense sensations.
    • Adapting to harmful stimuli might be harmful or even fatal.
💡 Example: People living in "Kera" / Addis Ababa stopped noticing the bad smell of that environment over time.

🔍 2.3 Perception

Perception is a meaning-making process. The major characteristics of the perceptual process include:

  1. Selectivity of perception (Attention)
  2. Form perception
  3. Depth perception
  4. Perceptual constancy
  5. Perceptual illusion

1. Selectivity of Perception (Attention)

Selectivity of perception refers to the perceptual process that selects certain inputs for inclusion in your conscious experience, or awareness, at any given time, ignoring others.

  • Focus & Margin: Our field of experience is divided into:
    • Focus: Events or stimuli that you perceive clearly.
    • Margin: Items or stimuli that you perceive dimly or vaguely.
  • Attention shifts constantly. What is in the focus of your attention one moment may enter the margin, and vice versa.

Factors Determining Attention:

Paying attention is a function of two primary sets of factors:

  • External Factors (Characteristics of the Object/Stimulus):
    • Size and Intensity: Bigger and brighter objects catch attention easily.
    • Repetition: Frequently occurring stimuli.
    • Novelty (Newness): Unique or new stimuli in the environment.
    • Movement: Moving objects are more likely to get noticed than stationary ones.
  • Internal Factors (Psychological State of the Observer):
    • Even with bright or moving stimuli, attention depends on psychological readiness.
    • Set or Expectancy: What a person expects to perceive.
    • Motives or Needs: Personal desires, drives, and physiological requirements at the time.

2. Form Perception

Visual sensations provide raw materials organized into meaningful patterns, shapes, forms, and concepts. To perceive forms, you need to distinguish a figure (an object) from its ground (surrounding background).

Components of Form Perception:

I. Contours in Form Perception: You are able to separate forms from the general ground only because you can perceive contours. Contours are formed whenever a marked difference occurs in the brightness or color of the background.

II. Organization in Form Perception (Gestalt Principles): When several objects are present in the visual field, we tend to perceive them as organized into patterns or groupings. As Gestalt psychologists stated: "The whole is more than the sum of its parts."

Gestalt Laws of Perceptual Organization:

  • Law of Proximity: Items close together in space or time tend to be perceived as belonging together or forming an organized group.
  • Law of Similarity: Similar items tend to be organized together (e.g., three dots arranged in a ring are seen as a triangle). Similarity competes with symmetry or good figure.
  • Law of Good Figure (Simplicity): The tendency to organize shapes into the simplest, most balanced, or symmetrical figures that include all parts (e.g., six dots arranged in a circle viewed as a hexagon or star).
  • Law of Continuity: The tendency to perceive a line that starts in one way as continuing smoothly in the same direction.
  • Law of Closure: Perceptual processes that organize the perceived world as complete by filling in gaps in stimulation to see complete forms.
  • Law of Common Fate: The tendency to group objects together that move or appear to move in the same direction (e.g., flocks of birds, herds of cattle, or children playing together).

Summary Table of Gestalt Laws

Law What It Means Example
Proximity Close items grouped together Cluster of dots spaced close together
Similarity Similar items grouped together Dots forming a triangle
Good Figure Organize into simple symmetrical shapes Dots forming a hexagon or star
Continuity Smooth, continuous lines Curved line continuing smoothly
Closure Fill in gaps to see complete shapes Incomplete circle seen as a full circle
Common Fate Objects that move together are grouped Flock of flying birds / Herd of cattle

3. Depth Perception

While form perception is our understanding of the world in two dimensions (height & width), depth perception is our judgment of the world in the third dimension (distance or depth of objects).

Depth perception relies on binocular cues (requiring two eyes) and monocular cues (requiring one eye).

A. Binocular Cues (Require both eyes):

  • Retinal Disparity: The degree of difference between the images focused on the two retinas. The closer the object, the greater the retinal disparity.
  • Convergence: The degree to which the eyes turn inward to focus on an object. The closer the object, the greater the convergence.

B. Monocular Cues (Require one eye):

Allows individuals with sight in one eye to retain accurate depth perception.

  • Accommodation: Change in the shape of the lens to focus an image on the retina. Closer objects require greater accommodation. Prolonged accommodation can alter depth perception.
  • Motion Parallax: The tendency to perceive ourselves as passing objects faster when they are closer to us than when they are farther away.
    • Objects closer than the point of fixation appear to move backward.
    • Objects beyond the point of fixation appear to move forward.
    • Greatly distant objects (e.g., sun or moon) always appear to move forward with the observer.
  • Pictorial Cues: Cues used by artists to create depth on a flat canvas:
    • Interposition (Overlap): An object that overlaps another appears closer.
    • Relative Size: If two objects are known to be equal size, the one casting a smaller retinal image is perceived as farther away.
    • Linear Perspective: Parallel lines appear to converge as they recede into the distance.
    • Elevation: Objects placed higher in the visual field appear farther away.
    • Shading Patterns (Light & Shadow): Shadowed areas recede, while illuminated areas stand out.
    • Aerial Perspective (Clarity): Closer objects appear clearer and sharper than distant, hazy ones.
    • Texture Gradient: Finer details are distinguishable on closer objects, whereas distant objects lose fine details.

4. Perceptual Constancies

Despite changes in retinal images regarding size, shape, and brightness, we perceive objects as stable due to perceptual constancy.

  • Size Constancy: Interprets a change in retinal image size as a change in distance rather than actual physical size (e.g., seeing hands at different distances as equal in size).
  • Shape Constancy: An object of known shape maintains its perceived normal shape regardless of viewing angle (e.g., an open door forms a trapezoid on the retina but is perceived as rectangular).
  • Brightness Constancy: Objects are perceived as having constant brightness despite variations in the amount of reflected light.

5. Perceptual Illusion

Illusions are misperceptions or false perceptions of physical objects.

  • Visual / Optical Illusions: Physical stimuli that consistently produce perceptual errors (e.g., Moon illusion, Müller-Lyer line length illusion).
  • Hallucinations: Sensory experiences and perceptions that occur without corresponding external reality (visual, auditory, etc.).
  • Delusions: Unfounded beliefs strongly held despite lack of evidence.
    • Delusion of Persecution: Belief that dangerous enemies are targeting or persecuting you.
    • Delusion of Grandeur: Belief that you are unusually important, a divine messenger, or central to world affairs.
    • Delusion of Reference: Tendency to interpret random events or external messages as directly directed at yourself.

🔮 Extrasensory Perception (ESP) or Paranormal Ability

ESP refers to perception that occurs independently of recognized sensory processes. It includes:

  • Telepathy: Mind-to-mind communication or reading the thoughts of others.
  • Clairvoyance: Perceiving events or objects without being physically present (supernormal awareness).
  • Precognition: The ability to perceive future events before they occur.
  • Mediumship: The ability to communicate with the spiritual realm and deceased individuals.
  • Clairgustance: The paranormal capacity to taste a substance without putting anything in the mouth.
